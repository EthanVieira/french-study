# Level B2: Vantage

**Prerequisite:** Milestone B1 reached (the last step of 03-B1.md ticked). The README methods still apply, and the B1 day continues in the heavier B2 form defined below.

**Goal at exit:** you can follow extended speech, the television news and a radio debate, films in standard French without subtitles and most natural conversation between native speakers, including the fast, elided French of a series like *Kaamelott*; read contemporary literary prose, a *Le Monde* opinion piece and an official letter with ease, and a nineteenth-century novel with the *passé simple* without noticing it; take part in discussion with fluency, argue a position, defend it under questioning and use the discourse markers (*enfin, bref, du coup, en fait, quand même, voilà*) that make speech sound French; and write clear, well-structured texts of 250 to 350 words in the right register: a *lettre formelle*, an *essai argumenté*, a *compte rendu*, a *critique*, a *courrier des lecteurs*. Vocabulary about 8000 words. Grammar is complete and mostly automatic, including the subjunctive, the agreement rules and the written tense system; spelling is a trained skill with a logged dictée error rate. Remaining work is register, nuance and error repair. This is the level of the DELF B2, of French universities' entry requirement and of most French-speaking workplaces.

**Time:** the dailies take about 2 hours 20 minutes a day (half of it the commute slot and free reading, which need no desk) and the steps add up to about 540 hours: about 1020 hours in all. At the planned pace, over 34 weeks, that is 2 hours 40 minutes of steps a day, 6 days a week; at one hour of steps a day the level takes about 90 weeks. Keep the order either way.

**What changes at B2** (Appendix A): everything is native material, with one coursebook beside it: *Édito B2* (Didier), twelve units in four sittings each over Weeks 1–26, two sittings in most weeks, for the authentic documents, the lexique and the DELF B2 tasks that the series and the press do not set; its DELF pages are the exam preparation of Block 4. Grammar is a complete pass through *Grammaire progressive du français, niveau avancé*, with *French Grammar and Usage* read chapter by chapter beside it and, in the last third, the hard chapters again with the exercise book *Practising French Grammar*. Spelling becomes a trained skill: a *Projet Voltaire* session and a dictée every week, with the Certificat Voltaire as the C1 target. *Dix pour cent* and *Kaamelott* replace *Fais pas ci, fais pas ça* as the two television spines, then *Le Bureau des légendes* and *Engrenages*. Literature is the centre: Voltaire, Camus, Sartre, Molière, Maupassant, Flaubert, Ernaux, Duras, Gary, Nothomb, Vercors, Mariama Bâ, Ionesco, Saint-Exupéry, Modiano, Slimani and Pagnol, each with its audiobook in the commute slot, and twenty-four poems and twelve chansons by heart. Non-fiction is the history of France read in Lorànt Deutsch's *Métronome* beside its France 5 series and *Secrets d'Histoire*. *Le Monde* is a daily from Block 0, with *Courrier international* and *Télérama* every week. Speaking adds debate, the DELF-style *exposé* and a weekly *revue de presse*. Writing reaches the five DELF B2 genres. Films go from eleven to sixteen, and French subtitles end in Week 18.

## The B2 day

Every study day: **the dailies, in the order of the table, then the current step**, the first unticked step in this file. One step is one sitting with one focus; a step too long for today is resumed tomorrow. The old weekly rhythm and the weekly habits are now individual steps placed in order inside each week: nothing runs in the background except the dailies.

| Daily | Minutes | Starts at | Ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anki: all due cards, then 20 new (half of them sentence cards) | 20 | A1-004 | never |
| Commute slot: the audiobook of the current book (chapters already read); on days without one, France Inter's *journal de 8h* or RFI's *Journal en français facile* | 30 | B2-007 | C1 changes its content |
| *Le Monde*, two articles (the lead story and one opinion piece), two harvested cards | 20 | B2-010 | C1 keeps it |
| AI voice conversation, topic fixed by the rule in B2-009 | 15 | B2-009 | never |
| The daily song, sung twice, and the current poem recited once | 5 | A1-040 | C1 makes it the daily poem or song |
| The journal, 150 words, corrected on Sundays | 15 | B2-011 | end of B2; C1 makes it the journal de lecture |
| Free reading, from the fixed list | 30 | B2-008 | never |

A week check passes when every step since the previous check is ticked and the dailies were done on at least six of the last seven days.

The steps that return every week are written as one line and use these patterns:

- **Monologue**: 5 minutes on the topic named (Appendix F part 2), Recording protocol, transcribed and corrected. Today's voice session is on this topic and tomorrow's is the matching debate motion from Appendix F part 3, with the assistant arguing the other side.
- **Dictée**: a text of 150–200 words, dictated audio, written in full in one pass and checked against the correction; errors counted per 100 words and by category (*accord, conjugaison, homophone, accent, lexique, ponctuation*), logged in `writing/dictees.md`. The source rotates in a fixed order, week by week: (1) *La Dictée d'Archibald* on TV5Monde, the next text at the highest level offered; (2) a Bescherelle online dictée for adults; (3) one of Bernard Pivot's dictées from the Dicos d'or, the *amateurs* text; (4) a Projet Voltaire dictée; then again from (1).
- **Poem**: the poem named (Appendix J), on Wikisource: read it three times aloud, look up every word, listen to one recorded reading (search the title with *lu par*), copy it by hand, learn it by heart in 20-line pieces, record yourself reciting it without the text. It is recited once a day in the daily-song slot until the next poem step. Write out the previous poem from memory first and check it.
- **Song**: write out last week's song from memory; then the next song on the Appendix J list: listen twice with the lyrics, look up every word, sing along 3 times. It is the daily song until the next song step.
- **Revue de presse**: a 3-minute recorded oral review of the week's five main stories from your *Le Monde* cards, in the manner of France Inter's morning *revue de presse*, without notes; listen back; note three errors.
- **Magazine**: odd weeks *Courrier international*, one dossier (two long articles translated from the world press), extensive, at most 6 lookups, a three-sentence French summary; even weeks *Télérama*, two *critiques* (one of a film, series or book you are on, one of your choice) and one *chronique*, with five words of critical vocabulary to Anki.
- **Long-form pattern** (the podcasts): the episode named, extensive first; then a written 8-sentence summary in French; then a second listen of the 5 minutes you understood least, pausing to write down every phrase you can catch; 8 cards.
- **Projet Voltaire session**: 30 minutes on the platform, the next modules in the order it proposes after its placement test; every rule you failed copied into the notebook with its example; the module and score logged in the margin.
- **Wikipedia history article**: one French Wikipedia article on a period, event or figure you met in *Métronome* or *Secrets d'Histoire*, extensive, 5 cards.

**Episode pattern** (*Dix pour cent*, *Le Bureau des légendes*, *Engrenages*): watch with French subtitles (Weeks 1–17) or without (Week 19 on), no pausing. Then one 8-minute scene three times: with subtitles, without, and pausing to shadow twelve lines. Six sentences in French on the plot and one on a term of the trade (the film business, intelligence, police and justice); 10 cards. Tracker note.

**Kaamelott pattern**: ten episodes (about 35 minutes) once through without pausing, French subtitles until Week 18. Then the three you understood least, again with subtitles, pausing; write down five lines that carry the joke in each; shadow the two fastest exchanges five times each; 5 cards of spoken expressions marked *fam.* The series is fast, elided and full of wordplay, which is exactly why it is here; do not expect to catch everything on the first pass.

**Book pattern** (B2 form): before, the French Wikipedia entries on the author and the work (lead sections) and the introduction given in the step; during, the audiobook chapter in the commute slot *after* you have read it where an audiobook exists; intensive on the first 20 pages of each book (at most 12 words per chapter to the notebook), extensive after that with at most 8 lookups per 30 pages; after, an essay of 300–400 words on the question the finish step sets, Correction protocol, filed in `writing/portfolio-B2.md`; a 10-minute AI seminar ("Fais-moi un séminaire de dix minutes sur ce livre : interroge-moi sur les personnages, la structure, la fin et ce que j'en pense, et contredis-moi"); five imitated sentences (copy five sentences you admired, then write five of your own on the same pattern, corrected); tracker row.

**GPA pattern**: read the chapters named (use the contents page of your edition; the step gives the chapter topics, not page numbers), copy every rule and example that was new into the notebook, do the exercises in pencil, check them against the *corrigés*, then write 20 own sentences on the theme's points and run the Correction protocol. **GU pattern**: read the chapter named, mark every rule you did not know, copy the ten most useful with the book's example and one of your own, then 10 sentences, corrected. **PFG pattern** (Weeks 25–31): reread the GU chapter, do every exercise for it in *Practising French Grammar*, check against the key, copy every wrong answer with its rule, then 20 sentences, corrected.

**HIST pattern** (*Métronome*): one chapter (one century, one metro station) a sitting, about 15 pages; 3 cards; a 4-sentence French summary; one date, one figure and one place from the chapter in the notebook. **SDH pattern** (*Secrets d'Histoire*): the episode named, extensive, auto-captions allowed until Week 18; ten dates or names in the notebook with one French sentence each; tracker.

**Film pattern**: read a French review first (*Télérama*, *Le Monde* or the French Wikipedia lead); watch with French subtitles (Weeks 1–17) or without (Week 19 on), no pausing; 150–200 words in French on the question the step asks, Correction protocol; tracker.

**Édito pattern** (ED, Weeks 1–26): a unit of *Édito B2* is four steps, one sitting each, two in most weeks. (a) *Documents*: every listening and video document of the unit with the LRS cycle (Method 1; the transcriptions are at the back of the livre or in the guide pédagogique), and every reading document intensively (unknown words in the notebook with their sentence, the comprehension questions answered in writing and checked); 8 cards. (b) *Grammar and lexique*: the unit's grammar and vocabulary pages; copy each rule into the notebook with the book's example and one of your own; do every exercise of the livre in writing and check it against the key (the *guide pédagogique*; where you have no key, the Correction protocol with the exercise's instruction pasted in); rewrite every wrong answer. Where the point is a GPA theme you have already done, treat the exercises as a test and reopen GPA for anything you miss; where it is one still to come, note the page for that week. (c) *Cahier and cards*: the cahier d'activités pages for the unit, in writing, checked against the *corrigés*, every wrong answer rewritten; then the unit's words to Anki by the card rules (at most 30, sentence cards where the book gives a sentence). (d) *DELF task*: the unit's *production écrite* (the task its production or DELF page sets, in the DELF B2 genre it names), 250–350 words closed book under the exam's hour, Correction protocol, filed in `writing/portfolio-B2.md` as the week's text; and its *production orale* (the *exposé* or the debate the unit sets) with the Recording protocol, 5 minutes, the AI taking the other side with the debate instruction of B2-006, transcribed and corrected; today's voice session is on the unit's theme. The comprehension parts of the unit's DELF pages are left untouched for Block 4 (B2-584, B2-649). Done when (d)'s corrections are in the notebook and the recording is saved. The steps name the units by number in the current edition; if yours has a different number of units, keep the pace of one unit every two weeks and merge or double the shortest so that the book ends in Week 26.

## Block 1: Weeks 1–9

### Week 1

- [ ] **B2-014** [Study · Deep · 75 min] GPA theme: the written past, *passé simple* and *passé antérieur*.
      Do: GPA pattern on the chapters on the past tenses and the *passé simple*. The *passé simple* in full, because from this week every novel uses it: *-er* verbs *je parlai, tu parlas, il parla, nous parlâmes, vous parlâtes, ils parlèrent*; *-ir* and most *-re* verbs *je finis, il finit, nous finîmes, ils finirent* / *je rendis, ils rendirent*; the *-u-* group *je fus, il fut, nous fûmes, ils furent* (être), *j'eus, il eut, ils eurent* (avoir), *il put, il sut, il voulut, il dut, il lut, il vécut, il connut, il crut, il reçut*; the *-i-* irregulars *il fit, il dit, il mit, il prit, il vit, il rit, il écrivit, il naquit*; *il vint, il tint, ils vinrent*. The *passé antérieur* (*quand il eut fini, dès qu'ils furent partis*) after *quand, dès que, aussitôt que, à peine ... que*. ## Appendix A: What changes at B2

1. **No learner material but one.** Every source is made for native speakers except the two grammars, the exercise book and *Édito B2*, whose documents are themselves press, radio and television and whose tasks are the DELF's; the coursebook is there for the method and the exam, not for shelter. When something is hard, the response is more of it, not something easier.
2. **Grammar twice, then in depth.** *Grammaire progressive du français, niveau avancé* gives you the rules in French with their French names (*la concordance des temps, le participe passé, la mise en relief, la proposition subordonnée, le registre soutenu*), one theme a week for twenty-four weeks, with the matching chapter of *French Grammar and Usage* beside it; then the seven hard chapters again with *Practising French Grammar* and its key. After Week 31, grammar work is error analysis only.
3. **Spelling is a skill, not a hope.** A *Projet Voltaire* session and a dictée every week, with the errors logged by category. French is the language of the dictée; a learner who writes *j'ai manger* or *les lettres que j'ai écrit* is marked down everywhere, in a job application as in the DELF. Your error rate should fall from about 6 per 100 words to 3 by the exit; the Certificat Voltaire, at C1, will put a number on it.
4. **The rhythm carries the level.** A grammar theme, a GU chapter, a Projet Voltaire session, a dictée, a monologue and a debate, a *revue de presse*, two Édito sittings, a bank text (the Édito production in Weeks 10–26), a magazine, a long podcast, two series steps, three book sittings, a poem or a song, and two articles of *Le Monde* every day. That is about 12 hours a week before any film, history or book-finishing. Do not trim it; trim the television if you must.
5. **Literature is the point.** Voltaire's *conte*, Camus's chronicle, Sartre's one act, Molière in prose, Maupassant's best novel, Flaubert's perfect story, Ernaux's flat prose, Duras's Goncourt, Gary's double Goncourt, Nothomb, Vercors's clandestine book, the first great West African novel by a woman, Ionesco, Saint-Exupéry, Modiano's Nobel prose, Slimani's Goncourt, Pagnol's Marseille. Every book is preceded by its author's Wikipedia entry and followed by an essay, a seminar and five imitated sentences; that is how French speakers are taught to read, and you will be asked.
6. **Two spines on screen.** *Dix pour cent* for the professional French of Paris at speed; *Kaamelott* for the fast, elided, joke-laden French that no textbook teaches and every French person quotes. Then *Le Bureau des légendes* for the French of offices and secrets, and *Engrenages* for the police and the courts.
7. **Subtitles end in Week 18.** After that, subtitles are a checking tool, not a viewing aid; the only exceptions are Rostand's verse and the Québec block.
8. **Speaking becomes argument.** The DELF-style *exposé* with its *document déclencheur*, debates against an assistant instructed to disagree, the *revue de presse* aloud every week. The discourse markers (*enfin, bref, du coup, en fait, quand même, voilà, bon, disons, justement, franchement*) are now a requirement, counted at the exit.
9. **Writing becomes the five genres.** *Lettre formelle*, *essai argumenté*, *compte rendu*, *critique*, *courrier des lecteurs*: the genres of the DELF B2 and of French public life, with the connectors and the plan the French school teaches; the bank sets them in Weeks 1–9 and 27–32, Édito's production tasks in between.

## Appendix B: B2 grammar checklist

1. **The written past**: *passé simple* in all groups and the common irregulars; *passé antérieur* after *quand, dès que, à peine ... que*; the division between *passé simple* (narrative), *passé composé* (speech, letters, press), *imparfait* (background) and *plus-que-parfait*.
2. **The subjunctive in full**: *présent* and *passé* produced, *imparfait* and *plus-que-parfait* recognised; every trigger class; *espérer* and *après que* with the indicative; the subjunctive after the superlative, *le seul*, the negated opinion and the relative of the sought-for thing.
3. **Concordance des temps**: backshift in reported speech with the time words shifted; the subjunctive sequence of modern French; the conditional as future-in-the-past, as hearsay (*le ministre aurait démissionné*) and as politeness.
4. **Si-clauses**: the three types and the mixed type; never future or conditional after *si*; *eût-il, n'était, dût-il*; *à supposer que, pour peu que, au cas où, à moins que ... ne, sinon, faute de quoi*; the gerund as condition.
5. **Participe présent, gérondif, adjectif verbal**: invariable participle, agreeing adjective, *en* + *-ant* with the main subject; the spelling pairs (*fatigant / fatiguant, convaincant / convainquant, précédent / précédant*); absolute clauses.
6. **Agreement in full**: participles with *être*, with a preceding direct object, with pronominal verbs (direct and indirect reflexive), with *en*, with *faire* and *laisser* + infinitive, with verbs of perception; *demi, nu, tout, même, cent, vingt, mille*; colour adjectives; the 1990 rectifications known as alternatives.
7. **Pronouns in full**: the order before and after the verb; *le* neutre; *y / en* against the stressed pronoun for people; *soi*; *ce / cela / ça / ceci*; *celui-ci / celui de / celui qui*; *ce qui / ce que / ce dont / ce à quoi*.
8. **Relatives in all forms**: *dont* in all its uses; *lequel* after prepositions and *duquel* where *dont* cannot reach; *où* of time; *quoi* and *quiconque*; the relative with the subjunctive; restrictive against explanatory and the comma.
9. **Mise en relief and dislocation**: the clefts *c'est ... qui / que, ce qui ..., c'est*; left and right dislocation in speech; the written inversion after *peut-être, aussi, à peine, sans doute*.
10. **Nominal style**: the nominalising suffixes; the support verbs (*procéder à, faire l'objet de, mettre en œuvre, faire preuve de, tenir compte de*); administrative French unpacked into plain French.
11. **The connectors of the dissertation** by function and register, with *or*, *en effet* and *certes ... mais* used correctly; the plan (*introduction, thèse, antithèse, synthèse, conclusion*).
12. **Register**: *soutenu / courant / familier / argot / verlan* with a vocabulary triplet for twenty everyday words; questions in three forms; *ne* and *on*; *tu / vous* and how to move between them; swearing recognised.
13. **Concession and opposition**: *bien que, quoique, qui que, quoi que, où que, quel que, si ... que* with the subjunctive; *alors que, tandis que, même si* with the indicative; *avoir beau*; *tout en*; *malgré, en dépit de*; *quand même* in all its uses.
14. **Condition and hypothesis beyond si**: *à condition que, pourvu que, à moins que ... ne, pour peu que* (subjunctive); *au cas où* (conditional); *selon que* (indicative); the *ne explétif*.
15. **Cause, consequence, purpose**: *puisque, comme, étant donné que, du fait que, non que* (subjunctive); *si bien que, de sorte que* (both moods by meaning), *au point que, tellement ... que, d'où, du coup*; *pour que, afin que, de peur que ... ne*; *pour* + past infinitive.
16. **Prepositions in depth**: *à / de* after verbs, adjectives and nouns; the verbs with no preposition where English has one and the reverse; *en / dans*; places; *par / de* with the agent; the prepositional phrases of written French (*au sein de, à l'issue de, à l'égard de, quant à, au détriment de, faute de*); 150 verb-preposition pairs.
17. **Articles in negation and quantity**: *de* after a negation, before a plural adjective and after quantity; *bien des, la plupart des*; the partitive against the definite; the article omitted and the cases where French keeps it.
18. **Gender and number**: gender by suffix with the exceptions; the gender pairs (*le / la livre, tour, poste, mode, voile, manche, mémoire, critique*); feminine professions; the plurals in *-aux, -eux, -oux*; compound nouns in both spellings.
19. **Accents and spelling**: *é / è / ê*, the circumflex and its 1990 exceptions, the *tréma* and the *cédille*; thirty homophone pairs; *-er / -é / -ez*; *-ai / -ais*; adverbs in *-amment / -emment*; *tout, même, quelque, demi, leur*; capitals.
20. **Punctuation and typography**: the space before *; : ! ?*, the *guillemets* with their spaces, the *tiret* of dialogue, the comma rules, capitals on accented letters and in titles, the abbreviations, the thin space in numbers, the decimal comma, *10 %*, *1er / 2e*, dates.
21. **The tense system in narrative and report**: the narrative and historical present, the *imparfait pittoresque*, the *futur antérieur* of conjecture, the past conditional, *venir de / être en train de / aller* in the past; free indirect style recognised.
22. **Passive and impersonal**: *par / de* with the agent; only a direct object becomes a subject; *on*, the pronominal passive, *se faire / se voir / se laisser* + infinitive; *il s'agit de, il se peut que, il semble que / il me semble que, il convient de, il est* + adjective + *de / que*.
23. **The infinitive**: as subject, after every preposition, past infinitive, *faire / laisser* + infinitive with their pronouns, the verbs of perception, *à / de* before an infinitive, *maison à vendre*.
24. **Interrogation, negation, exclamation** in three registers: complex inversion; *que / qu'est-ce que / quoi*; the full negative set with *ne ... que* and *ni ... ni*; *personne / rien / aucun / nul* as subjects; the *ne* alone of written French; *Que c'est beau ! Comme ... ! Quel ... ! Qu'est-ce que ... !*
25. **Discourse markers, active**: *enfin, bref, du coup, en fait, quand même, voilà, bon, ben, disons, justement, franchement, c'est-à-dire, genre* (young), *tu vois, t'sais* (Québec), *hein, quoi* (final), *allez, bon courage*; and the register of each.

## Appendix E: B2 exit reading test

**Faut-il encore apprendre l'orthographe ?**

Chaque année, à la rentrée, le même débat revient dans les colonnes des journaux : le niveau en orthographe des élèves baisse, les copies du bac en témoignent, et une dictée de 1987 refaite aujourd'hui donnerait, paraît-il, deux fois plus de fautes. On s'indigne, on accuse l'école, les écrans, la méthode globale ou les correcteurs automatiques, puis on passe à autre chose jusqu'à la rentrée suivante. Je voudrais, pour une fois, prendre la question par l'autre bout : à quoi sert l'orthographe, et vaut-elle le temps qu'on lui consacre ?

Commençons par reconnaître ce que ses défenseurs admettent rarement. L'orthographe française est, pour une large part, arbitraire. Le *s* de *temps* ne se prononce pas, le participe passé s'accorde avec un complément qu'il faut aller chercher avant le verbe, et *nénuphar* a pris un *ph* par erreur d'étymologie. Aucune de ces règles ne rend la langue plus claire ; elles sont le produit de l'histoire, pas de la logique. Un enfant qui écrit *ognon* n'est pas moins intelligent qu'un autre, et depuis 1990 il n'a même plus tort.

Il n'en reste pas moins que l'orthographe remplit une fonction sociale que l'on aurait tort de nier. Dans un pays où le concours, la lettre de motivation et le courrier administratif décident encore de beaucoup de vies, une faute d'accord se paie cher. Les recruteurs l'avouent sans détour : à compétences égales, la candidature qui comporte des fautes est écartée. On peut trouver cela injuste ; on ne peut pas faire comme si cela n'existait pas. Apprendre l'orthographe, c'est donc d'abord se donner les moyens de ne pas être jugé sur elle.

Il y a ensuite un argument que les pédagogues connaissent bien et que le grand public ignore : l'orthographe s'apprend avec la lecture, et la lecture s'apprend avec l'orthographe. L'élève qui distingue *a* de *à* lit plus vite, parce qu'il n'a pas à hésiter ; celui qui lit beaucoup finit par voir les mots avant de les penser. Les deux compétences se nourrissent l'une l'autre, et c'est pourquoi les pays qui ont allégé l'enseignement de la langue écrite ont vu reculer, quelques années plus tard, la compréhension des textes.

Reste la question du temps. Les heures de français ont diminué d'un quart en cinquante ans, et l'on demande à l'école d'enseigner par ailleurs l'informatique, l'anglais dès le primaire et l'éducation aux médias. Il faudra donc choisir, et je ne crois pas qu'il faille choisir la dictée hebdomadaire contre tout le reste. Mais entre la dictée du samedi et l'abandon pur et simple, il existe une voie : enseigner les règles qui comptent, celles qui portent sur le sens, et laisser aux dictionnaires les exceptions que personne ne retient.

Au fond, le débat sur l'orthographe est un débat sur ce que nous attendons de l'école : qu'elle fabrique des citoyens capables de lire un contrat et d'écrire une lettre, ou qu'elle trie les enfants selon leur mémoire des pièges. Si c'est la première réponse qui l'emporte, et je le souhaite, alors oui, il faut encore apprendre l'orthographe, mais pas n'importe laquelle, et pas à n'importe quel prix.

*Hélène Marchand, Rennes*

La grève a entraîné l'annulation de nombreux trains. / À cause de la grève, de nombreux trains ont été annulés. 4. Il s'est excusé d'être arrivé en retard. / Il a présenté ses excuses pour son retard. 5. En raison de la pluie, le concert a été déplacé à l'intérieur. / À cause de la pluie, on a déplacé le concert en salle. 6. Je doute qu'ils acceptent l'offre. 7. La situation est pire qu'il y a un an. / ... pire qu'elle ne l'était il y a un an. 8. Personne ne semble savoir qui est responsable. 9. Elle est l'une des rares qui osent parler. / C'est l'une des seules à oser prendre la parole. 10. Le pont est fermé depuis l'accident. 11. C'est une question de priorités, pas d'argent. 12. Il ne sort presque plus. / Il ne sort plus guère.

**Part 3 (Milestone B2.3, Week 27):**

1. The company denies having broken any rules. 2. On the one hand it saves money; on the other, it costs jobs. 3. Not a single one of the proposals was adopted. 4. She would have called if she had had your number. 5. The report is based on interviews with 200 residents. 6. It was only later that I realised what she meant. 7. The council is considering banning cars from the centre. 8. He acted against his doctor's advice. 9. The results are to be published in the spring. 10. Much of what was said was already known. 11. The film is worth seeing despite its length. 12. I was about to leave when the phone rang.

**Key:** 1. L'entreprise nie avoir enfreint la moindre règle. / La société dément avoir violé une quelconque règle. 2. D'une part, cela fait économiser de l'argent ; d'autre part, cela coûte des emplois. 3. Pas une seule des propositions n'a été adoptée. 4. Elle aurait appelé si elle avait eu ton numéro. 5. Le rapport s'appuie sur des entretiens avec deux cents habitants. / Le rapport se fonde sur deux cents entretiens avec des riverains. 6. Ce n'est que plus tard que j'ai compris ce qu'elle voulait dire. 7. La mairie envisage d'interdire les voitures dans le centre. / Le conseil municipal étudie la possibilité d'interdire le centre aux voitures. 8. Il a agi contre l'avis de son médecin. 9. Les résultats doivent être publiés au printemps. / Les résultats seront publiés au printemps. 10. Une grande partie de ce qui a été dit était déjà connue. 11. Le film vaut la peine d'être vu malgré sa longueur. / Le film mérite d'être vu en dépit de sa durée. 12. J'étais sur le point de partir quand le téléphone a sonné.

## Appendix I: Writing task bank

250–350 words unless stated. Closed book. Correction protocol. Clean copies with error counts to `writing/portfolio-B2.md`. Tasks 1–9 are the texts of Weeks 1–9; tasks 1–6 are done again in Weeks 27–32 and compared. Tasks 10–20 are the milestone and exit tests (6, 13 and 15 are named there), the Week 31 DELF practice (12, 15, 16) and the reserve: when an Édito production task cannot be done as set (no posting, no event to hand), take the next unused task here instead. The five DELF B2 genres are marked; every task uses one of them.

1. A *courrier des lecteurs* to *Le Monde* on a current story of your choice.
2. A *lettre formelle* of complaint to a company about a service, citing dates, what was promised and what you demand, with the conventions in full (*Madame, Monsieur, ... Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées*).
3. A *lettre de motivation* for a real job posting you found this week (with the posting saved).
4. A *compte rendu* of a *journal de 8h*: the stories, the sources, the open questions.
5. A *critique* of *Les Tontons flingueurs* or *Mon oncle* for a newspaper.
6. A *lettre formelle* (*réclamation*) to a *mairie* over a decision, referring to the decision's date and the *voies de recours*.
7. A portrait of Albert Camus for a library newsletter.
8. An *essai argumenté*: *Qu'est-ce que l'apéro, au fond ?*
9. A contribution to an online debate on an immigration story from this week's news, arguing one side fairly (*essai argumenté*).
10. An email to your bank or insurer disputing a charge (*lettre formelle*).
11. A *compte rendu* of an *Affaires sensibles* episode presenting the facts and the questions.
12. A *courrier des lecteurs* replying to a specific *tribune*, quoting it.
13. A *courrier des lecteurs* replying to a real opinion piece from this week's *Le Monde* (300 words).
14. A text explaining an event in French history to a newcomer, using *Métronome* and *Secrets d'Histoire* (*compte rendu*).
15. An *essai argumenté* (350 words) on a topic of your choice: *introduction* with *accroche, problématique* and *plan*, two or three parts, *conclusion* with *ouverture*.
16. A personal essay: *Mon rapport à la langue française* (350 words).
17. A *critique* of a French film for readers who have never seen French cinema.
18. A procedural text: how to do something practical in France (*s'inscrire à la mairie, obtenir une carte Vitale, faire sa déclaration de revenus*) with the impersonal *on* and *il faut* (*compte rendu*).
19. A *critique* of *La Vie devant soi* or *Bel-Ami*.
20. A memo to colleagues proposing a change at work, with pros, cons and a recommendation (*lettre formelle*).
