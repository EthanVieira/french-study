# Level C2: Mastery

**Prerequisite:** Milestone C1 reached (the last step of 05-C1.md ticked). The README methods still apply; the day continues in the C2 form below.

**Goal at exit:** you use French like an educated native speaker who reads. You can follow anything spoken in any standard or regional variety at any speed, including a Devos sketch, a *séance de questions au gouvernement* at the Assemblée nationale and the dialogue of a 1930s film; read any French text from the *Chanson de Roland*, Villon and Montaigne in their own spelling to this year's *rentrée littéraire*, with the history of the language in your ear; write in any genre, including literary prose, satire, the formal speech and the academic article, at a level an editor would print with light changes; speak for twenty minutes without notes on a specialised subject, moderate a debate, give a wedding *discours*, and switch register and regional features for effect; translate between English and French in both directions; and proofread French to publication standard with the *signes de correction*. Vocabulary above 18,000 words, including the older and regional vocabulary the canon needs. The DALF C2 is the highest official diploma; this level takes its sample paper in full and adds a portfolio for everything the exam does not measure.

**Time:** the dailies take about 2 hours 25 minutes a day (half of it the commute slot and free reading, which need no desk) and the steps add up to about 680 hours: about 1200 hours in all. At the planned pace, over 36 weeks, that is 3 hours 10 minutes of steps a day, 6 days a week; at one hour of steps a day the level takes about 113 weeks. Keep the order either way.

**What changes at C2** (Appendix A): the canon is read from its beginning and in its own spelling: the *Roland* and Marie de France, Chrétien, Villon, Rabelais, Montaigne in the 1595 text, the Pléiade poets, Corneille and Molière, the moralists, Saint-Simon, Voltaire and Rousseau, Chateaubriand, *Les Misérables* in full, Balzac, Zola, Mallarmé and Lautréamont, Céline, Gide, Claude Simon, and the Francophone and contemporary books between them. Language study becomes linguistics, language history, rhetoric, translation, proofreading and pastiche. Listening reaches the archive: *Apostrophes*, *Cinq colonnes à la une*, *Les Dossiers de l'écran*, *Au théâtre ce soir*, the radio drama of France Culture, the films of the 1930s–60s, the Assemblée, the comedians from Devos to Blanche Gardin. Speaking becomes performance: the *conférence*, the *discours*, the podcast. Writing becomes literature. And because the archive spine looks backwards, three things keep you in the present: every week has a step with an episode of a contemporary series beside the INA, and a step with today's comedians or *Affaires sensibles*; free reading, a daily, takes the big novels the canon skipped (Proust's *Recherche* from volume 2 to the end, Balzac, Stendhal's *Chartreuse*, Flaubert's *Éducation sentimentale*, Zola, Simon, Modiano, the living novel and the latest Goncourt); and the commute slot carries the audiobooks.

## The C2 day

Every study day: **the dailies, in the order of the table, then the current step**, the first unticked step in this file. One step is one sitting with one focus; a step too long for today is resumed tomorrow. The old weekly rhythm is now individual steps placed in order inside each week: nothing runs in the background except the dailies.

| Daily | Minutes | Starts at | Ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anki: all due cards, then 20 new (older vocabulary, collocations, register sets) | 20 | A1-004 | 10 new a day after the exit |
| The Académie's *Dire, ne pas dire* entry or the day's *Dictionnaire de l'Académie* entry read aloud, then 5 minutes of shadowing a France Inter or France Culture presenter | 10 | C2-011 | never |
| Commute slot: the audiobook of the current book | 30 | C2-006 | never |
| *Le Monde*: the lead story and one opinion column; two harvested cards | 20 | C2-009 | never |
| AI voice, mode by the four-day cycle in C2-008 (seminar, moderated debate between two AI guests, interview as the expert, casual register with slang) | 15 | C2-008 | never |
| The daily poem or chanson, recited twice | 5 | C1 | never |
| Journal de lecture, 150 words with one stylistic observation and one imitated sentence | 15 | C2-010 | never |
| Free reading, from the fixed C2 list | 30 | C2-007 | never |

A week check passes when every step since the previous check is ticked and the dailies were done on at least six of the last seven days.

The steps that return every week are written as one line and use these patterns:

- **Archive-radio pattern** (*Les Nuits de France Culture*, the programme that rebroadcasts the France Culture archive, on radiofrance.fr and in podcast apps): the next episode working forward from the oldest available; a three-sentence summary; one new term to Anki.
- **Assemblée pattern**: 30 minutes of a sitting on videos.assemblee-nationale.fr (the Tuesday or Wednesday *questions au gouvernement*, or a *discussion générale* on a bill); then the same passage in the *compte rendu intégral* (assemblee-nationale.fr, and in the *Journal officiel*, *Débats parlementaires*); note five differences between what was said and what was transcribed. Québec option: the *période de questions* of the Assemblée nationale du Québec with its *Journal des débats*, once a month in place of Paris.
- **Long programme**: the newest episode of the programme named, extensive, then an 8-sentence summary.
- **Comedian pattern**: a sketch, a show segment or a special of 30 to 45 minutes by the comedian named, no subtitles; ten puns or expressions explained in French to the assistant, which rates the explanations; the ones you could not explain go in the notebook.
- **Devos pattern**: one Raymond Devos sketch (INA, or the text in *Matière à rire*): hear it twice, transcribe it, mark every mechanism (homophony, an idiom taken literally, a syntactic ambiguity, a logical loop); then write a six-line variant on the same mechanism and have the pastiche judge rate it.
- **Translation pattern**: 300 words, 45 minutes, the direction alternating by week (odd weeks English into French from the list in Appendix K, even weeks French into English from the book you are reading); compare with the printed translation where one exists and with the comparator instruction; list five places where the translator chose differently and why.
- **Proofreading pattern**: the error-inserter corrupts one of your own 600-word bank texts with 20 errors (spelling, accents, agreement, punctuation, typography, lexis, register, sense); proofread on paper with the *signes de correction*; check the key; then an Antidote pass on the clean copy; log the score.
- **Dictée pattern**: one dictée from the Pivot archive (Appendix K), read to you by the AI voice in three passes at dictation speed, or heard in the INA recording where one exists; correct against the text; log the error count.
- **Performance**: a 12-minute *conférence* without notes, a 5-minute *discours* for an occasion, or a 10-minute podcast segment, as the step names; recorded, transcribed and edited. The week's poem is recited at the end of the recording.
- **Poem or chanson**: write out last week's from memory; then the next item of Appendix J: read it aloud three times, look up every word, learn the first half now. It is the daily poem until the next poem step.
- **Series pattern** (C2 form): no subtitles; nothing written beyond a sentence in the tracker and five expressions you would use. The order of series is fixed in the first series step and in Appendix K; each series starts straight after the last.

**Book pattern** (C2 form): (1) before: the French Wikipédia entry, LIT (the *Lagarde et Michard* pages on the author), and the edition's introduction (every Pléiade, GF and Folio classique edition has one); (2) during: older texts read with the TLFi or Godefroy open and the facsimile beside the modern edition where one exists on Gallica or Wikisource; the audiobook where one exists (Litterature audio, Audible, the France Culture readings); extensive with at most 5 lookups per 30 pages, marking sentences to imitate; (3) after: a 600-word essay (not a school *commentaire*: an argued reading with a thesis), an editor pass, a 10-minute seminar with the assistant playing a literature professor, and five imitated sentences; then the relevant *Apostrophes* or *La Grande Librairie* episode where one exists, in the archive television step after your essay is written, with two things it saw that you did not.

**Archive pattern** (*Apostrophes* and the INA programmes, the films of the 1930s–60s): no subtitles; note ten words or expressions that have changed or gone since; look each up in the TLFi with its date, and in Frantext (or the TLFi's examples) for its last common use.

## Appendix A: What changes at C2

1. **The canon from the beginning, in its own spelling.** The *Roland* and Marie de France in Old French with the translation beside, Chrétien, Villon in Middle French, Rabelais and Montaigne in the spelling of 1534 and 1595, the Pléiade poets, then the classical and modern centuries to Céline and Claude Simon, with the Francophone and contemporary books between them. Every book gets a scholarly introduction, an essay with a thesis, and a seminar. By the end you can read any French text since 1100 without a modernised edition, with Godefroy or the TLFi open.
2. **Language study is linguistics and craft.** *Le Bon Usage* and the *Grammaire méthodique* for the system, the Académie's *Dire, ne pas dire* for the norm as it is argued today, Walter and Rey for the history, the nine editions of the *Dictionnaire de l'Académie*, Littré, the TLFi, Godefroy and Frantext for older text, the speech archive for rhetoric, and then translation, proofreading with the *signes de correction* and pastiche as tests of control.
3. **The archive.** *Apostrophes*, *Cinq colonnes à la une*, *Les Dossiers de l'écran*, *Au théâtre ce soir*, *Lectures pour tous*, France Culture's *Fictions* and *Les Maîtres du mystère*, and the films of the 1930s–60s give you the French of the twentieth century as it was spoken; Devos, Desproges, Coluche, Bourvil, Fernandel, Les Inconnus, Les Nuls, Les Deschiens and the Guignols give you wordplay at its fastest; the Assemblée gives you public speech at length, with the *compte rendu intégral* to check against.
4. **Performance.** Twelve-minute *conférences* without notes, occasional speeches in the French tradition (the *discours de mariage*, the *pot de départ*, the *éloge funèbre*, the *discours de réception* on a single word), podcast segments, moderation, pastiche read aloud. Fluency is assumed; the target is command.
5. **Literature as output.** The *nouvelle*, the essay in the tradition of Montaigne, Alain and Barthes, the comic *chronique* in the manner of Desproges, the *essai* on language. The editor instruction's verdict "publishable with light edits" is the standard.
6. **Translation both ways** against printed translations: Lydia Davis's Flaubert, Scott Moncrieff's Proust, Rose's and Donougher's Hugo, Manheim's Céline, and the Folio translations of Austen, Orwell, Joyce and Woolf into French. Nothing shows the gap between languages as exactly.
7. **The varieties as things you understand and can imitate.** Québec, Belgium, Switzerland, Africa and the Antilles were met at C1; at C2 you read Ducharme, Chamoiseau, Kourouma and Kateb Yacine, watch *District 31*, *La Trêve*, *Les Ordres* and *Mandabi*, and can switch features for effect.
8. **The present beside the archive.** A contemporary series every week, today's comedians and *Le Floodcast*, *Le Canard enchaîné* and *Le Monde* in full, the *rentrée littéraire* and the latest Goncourt, so that the archive does not leave you fluent in 1975.
9. **The DALF C2.** The one official test above C1; the full sample paper under time twice, marked by the official grids, at a margin well above the pass mark. Beside it, the Certificat Voltaire at its top band and Antidote as your own proofreader.
10. **Portfolio beside certificate.** The exam does not test the *conférence*, the *discours*, the translation, the proofread or the archive ear. The exit check is designed so that every claim in the CEFR C2 descriptors is backed by a recording or a document you could show.
11. **Afterwards.** Ten cards a day, the *journal de lecture*, one book a month, and the habit of noticing. The level does not end; it becomes how you live in the language.

## Appendix B: C2 language checklist

1. **Information structure**: first-constituent choice explained for every sentence; *thème* and *propos*; dislocation and *clivage*; the presentatives; inversion after an initial adverb; the exact conditions of subject inversion in writing.
2. **Discourse particles**: *bon, ben, enfin, quoi, hein, voilà, du coup, en fait, bref, disons, quand même, d'ailleurs, or, certes, soit* in their BU senses, combined, with the right stress and intonation; the *ne* alone and the *ne explétif*.
3. **Determination in depth**: the zero article, the partitive under negation and quantity, generic *le/les* against *un*, the article with body parts, *ce/il*, proper names with article, *tout, même, quelque, tel*.
4. **Complementation**: which verbs take a *que*-clause, the infinitive with *à*, *de* or nothing, the indirect question, and with what meaning difference; subjunctive selection with every class; the literary *imparfait* and *plus-que-parfait du subjonctif*; *fût-ce*, *dût-il*.
5. **Aspect and tense in narrative art**: the *passé simple* against the *passé composé* in narrative, the historical present, the free indirect style and its tense logic, the *imparfait* as camera, the *passé antérieur* and the *surcomposé*.
6. **Passives and impersonals in every register**, including the administrative (*il sera procédé*, *il est fait obligation*), the archaic and the literary; *on* in all its values.
7. **Older morphology, receptive**: the two-case declension, *-oit* for *-ait*, the *s* long, *y* for *i*, *&*, the enclitic forms (*jel*, *nel*), *moult*, *icelui*, *ains*, *oncques*; Villon's Middle French read at sight; the 1595 spelling read at sight; Old French with a crib.
8. **Language history**: the periods, the sound changes, the loss of declension, Villers-Cotterêts, Malherbe and Vaugelas, the Académie's editions, 1835, 1990, the norm debates, and the ability to date a text by its language within thirty years.
9. **Dialect and sociolect**: the features of Québec, Belgian, Swiss, African and Antillean French, Paris high and *populaire*, *verlan* and the *argot des cités*, youth register, the *accent du Midi*, and the ability to imitate two varieties recognisably.
10. **The regional languages and the creoles**: Occitan, Breton, Alsatian, Corsican, Basque and the *langues d'oïl* placed and recognised; a page of Antillean or Haitian creole read with the French beside it.
11. **Rhetoric**: the classical apparatus in French terms (*anaphore, période, prétérition, hypotypose, chiasme*); the conventions of every French occasional speech; moderation; the *vœux*; the *discours de réception*; the comic *chronique*.
12. **Humour**: pun mechanisms, Devos's logic, Desproges's false erudition, Coluche's *beauf* voice, the *Guignols*' puppets, *Le Canard*'s *calembours*, *Le Gorafi*'s conventions, the *second degré* and the *pince-sans-rire* as national registers.
13. **Register at will**, including deliberate breach for effect, archaism for irony, and the *langue administrative* as parody.
14. **Genre command**: *nouvelle, essai, tribune, éditorial, critique, nécrologie, discours, portrait, reportage, contribution à une consultation, rapport, compte rendu, résumé, sous-titre, note du traducteur, chronique*.
15. **Style analysis and pastiche**: the ability to name an author from a page and to write a page that names itself.
16. **Translation**: fidelity, idiom, rhythm; subtitle norms; the systematic asymmetries between French and English (the *on*, the subjunctive, the gerund against the *participe présent*, sentence length, the nominal style, the *faux amis*, the tenses of narrative).
17. **Proofreading**: the *signes de correction*, the typographic rules of the Imprimerie nationale, the eye; 40 errors in 1500 words caught with at most one miss.
18. **Editing**: structure, cutting, sharpening a thesis, killing nominalisation and *ce dernier*, rhythm.
19. **Idiom and quotation**: 300 idioms; the *citations* French speakers quote (La Fontaine, Molière, Pascal, Hugo, Rimbaud, Prévert, Audiard, Coluche, Devos, *Les Tontons flingueurs*, *Kaamelott*); proverbs.
20. **Older and regional vocabulary**: 500 items from the canon with their 1694 or Littré senses; 200 regional items.
21. **Prosody**: newsreader, lecturer, conversational and comic deliveries distinguishable on tape; the *e muet*, liaison and linking native-like in every register, including the liaisons a careful speaker makes and the ones nobody makes.
22. **Discourse in speech**: moderation phrases, turn management in a three-way debate, the art of the *incise*, backchannels in the right variety.
23. **Names of things, complete**: institutions, offices, laws, historical events, literary periods, the canon's titles and dates, in France and across the Francophonie.
24. **Self-editing**: every text two passes; every recording listened to once; the repair list maintained; Antidote as a last pass, never a first.
25. **Judgement**: knowing which French to use for which room, and when to say less.

## Appendix E: C2 exit reading test

**La langue qu'on cite**

Il y a un français que personne ne parle plus, mais que tous ceux qui ont été à l'école ici portent sur eux. C'est celui des fables de La Fontaine, des vers de Racine, des répliques de Molière, des dialogues d'Audiard et de ces phrases de Coluche ou de Devos qu'on entend citer à n'importe quelle table pour peu qu'on attende assez longtemps. Ce n'est pas une langue qu'on apprend ; c'est une langue dont on hérite, et comme la plupart des héritiers, nous ne prêtons guère attention à ce qui nous est échu.

On s'en aperçoit quand elle manque à quelqu'un. J'ai eu, il y a des années, un collègue arrivé en France à l'âge adulte et dont le français, à tout point de vue mesurable, valait mieux que le mien : plus correct, plus précis, débarrassé des tics et des restes d'accent que je traîne moi-même. Et pourtant il manquait quelque chose. Quand, à la table du déjeuner, quelqu'un disait « les cons, ça ose tout » et qu'un autre enchaînait « c'est même à ça qu'on les reconnaît », et que tout le monde riait, il comprenait les mots, mais pas le rire. Il ne savait pas qu'on venait de citer une réplique que toute la table avait entendue enfant, et que le rire ne venait pas du mot d'esprit mais de la reconnaissance.

On pourrait appeler cela une injustice, et c'en est une à certains égards. La langue héritée est une forme de capital qui se distribue par la naissance et par l'école, non par le mérite. Mais c'est en même temps le seul capital qui croît quand on le partage. Chaque fois que mon collègue demandait ce que cela avait de drôle et qu'on le lui expliquait, il en sortait un peu plus riche, et nous aussi, parce qu'il est impossible d'expliquer pourquoi « les cons, ça ose tout » fait rire sans se demander ce qu'au juste on nous a transmis, et qui.

C'est là que l'histoire de la littérature prend une importance qu'on lui reconnaît rarement. On ne lit pas Molière pour savoir ce qui s'est passé en 1664, ni, soit dit avec respect, parce que *Tartuffe* serait la meilleure pièce du monde. On le lit parce que la manière dont les Français se moquent depuis trois siècles de celui qui prêche trop et croit trop peu est encore notre manière de nous moquer. Harpagon est présent dans toute discussion sur l'argent. Monsieur Jourdain, dans toute discussion sur ce qu'on sait sans le savoir. Ce n'est pas de l'histoire littéraire ; c'est le mode d'emploi de la société où l'on vit.

Je me permets donc de proposer une chose qui sonne vieux jeu : que celui qui veut apprendre le français pour de bon ne se contente pas du français qu'on parle, mais s'approprie aussi le français qu'on cite. C'est un travail plus long, et ce n'est pas celui que les écoles de langues ont le temps de faire. Mais c'est le seul chemin vers le rire de la table du déjeuner, et le rire est, en fin de compte, la preuve la plus sûre qu'on est arrivé chez soi dans une langue.

Mon collègue a passé cette épreuve au bout de quelques années. Cela s'est produit un jour où l'un de nous, arrivé en retard à une réunion, s'excusait en expliquant que le train avait eu du retard et qu'il n'avait rien pu faire ; et lui, sans lever les yeux de ses papiers, a dit : « Ça ose tout. » Nous avons ri, il a ri, et personne n'a rien eu à expliquer. C'est même à ça qu'on les reconnaît.

Questions (répondez en français ; les questions 7 à 10 demandent une interprétation) :

1. Qu'entend l'auteur par « la langue qu'on cite » ?
2. Comment était le français du collègue, mesuré objectivement ?
3. Que manquait-il au collègue à la table du déjeuner, et d'où venait le rire ?
4. Pourquoi l'auteur parle-t-il d'injustice, et quel est le contre-argument ?
5. Quelle est, selon l'auteur, la vraie raison de lire Molière ?
6. Que propose l'auteur à celui qui veut apprendre le français, au-delà de la langue parlée ?
7. Quel est le genre du texte, et quels traits le montrent ?
8. Expliquez les deux citations (« les cons, ça ose tout » et « c'est même à ça qu'on les reconnaît ») et leur fonction dans le texte.
9. Comment l'auteur se sert-il d'Harpagon et de Monsieur Jourdain ? De quel type d'argument s'agit-il ?

**Key:** 1. Le français partagé et cité (La Fontaine, Racine, Molière, Audiard, Coluche, Devos) qu'on reçoit par l'enfance et l'école, non par l'enseignement. 2.
