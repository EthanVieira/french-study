# Level A2: Waystage

**Prerequisite:** Milestone A1 reached (the last step of [01-A1.md](01-A1.md) ticked) and [README.md](README.md) methods still in use.

**Goal at exit:** you can handle routine situations (travel, shops, the pharmacy, housing, the doctor, restaurants) and short social exchanges, describe your background, surroundings, plans and past experiences in connected sentences, understand the main points of slow clear speech and learner podcasts on familiar topics, read short simple texts, graded news and a short book, and write connected texts of about 150 words (emails, descriptions, simple narratives). Vocabulary about 2000 words. You know the whole core grammar of French: the *passé composé* and the *imparfait* and how to choose between them, the *plus-que-parfait* on sight, the two futures and the conditional, the object pronouns with *y* and *en* in their order, the imperative with pronouns, the relative pronouns including *dont*, comparison, the pronominal verbs and the gerund, and you recognise the present subjunctive in its commonest frames and the *passé simple* on the page. Your pronunciation is stable enough that you are understood on the first try, with the nasal vowels, *u* against *ou* and the compulsory liaisons in place.

**Time:** the dailies take about 2 hours a day at this level (half of that is the comprehensible-input video and the commute slot, which need no desk) and the steps add up to about 220 hours: about 440 hours in all. At the planned pace, over 18 weeks, that is 2 hours of steps a day, 6 days a week; at one hour of steps a day the level takes about 36 weeks. Keep the order either way.

**What changes at A2** (read Appendix A first): the weight shifts from courses towards input. Language Transfer is finished. A slow learner podcast, graded real news for children from *1jour1actu*, a street-interview channel and the learner sitcom return as steps in every week; the comprehensible-input video moves up a level and up to 40 minutes a day; the commute slot becomes a story podcast; a free-reading habit and the *RFI* graded texts give you volume on the page; and *Un gars, une fille* becomes a weekly series from Week 13. Assimil *New French with Ease* joins *Complete French* as a second spine, and its "second wave" becomes a daily. You read your first real French book, *Le Petit Prince*. Speaking practice moves from repeating to producing, with an AI voice conversation habit from the first day and weekly recorded monologues.

**Patterns.** A one-line step that names one of these is done exactly as the pattern says and is finished when its last instruction is done.

- **Unit pattern (CF units 9–16):** four steps per unit, as at A1. (a) *Dialogue*, 50 min: the LRS cycle (all six stages) on every dialogue of the unit. (b) *Grammar and exercises*, 50 min: read the unit's language notes, copy each rule into the notebook with one example of your own, do every exercise of the unit in writing, check with the key, rewrite every wrong answer. (c) *Vocabulary and shadowing*, 40 min: every new word of the unit into Anki by the card rules (at most 25), then shadow the unit's audio live three times. (d) *8 sentences*, 25 min: eight original sentences using the unit's grammar and words, Correction protocol, corrected sentences copied by hand. Done when (d)'s corrections are in the notebook.
- **Assimil fast-pass pattern (lessons 1–49):** seven lessons per step, 60 min: LRS stages 1–3 on every dialogue (blind, read and look up, listen with text), read the notes and write into the notebook only what is new to you, do the two exercises of each lesson aloud, at most 10 cards per step. Lessons 50–100 get the full pattern.
- **Assimil lesson pattern (lessons 50–100):** one lesson per step, 25 min: the full LRS cycle on the dialogue (all six stages), the notes read and copied where new, both exercises in writing and checked against the key, at most 5 cards. Every seventh lesson is a revision lesson with no dialogue: it is done in the same step as the lesson before it (40 min), its exercises in writing.
- **Podcast pattern (POD):** the next episode of *Little Talk in Slow French* in order from the earliest (fallback: *Coffee Break French* season 2, in order). LRS stages 1–3, then write a 5-sentence English summary *before* reading the transcript, check it against the transcript and score yourself out of 5. Add 8 cards. From Week 7, add stage 4 (shadow one 60-second segment). 40 min.
- **InnerFrench pattern (IF):** the next *InnerFrench* episode in order from episode 1, the same pattern as POD, 8 cards. Hugo speaks only French, faster than the slow podcast, and repeats himself on purpose. 45 min. From Week 11 the two podcasts share one 50-minute step: POD blind only (listen twice, summary, check), IF in full.
- **TV5Monde rule:** the next two exercise series on the A2 list at apprendre.tv5monde.com, in order, 45 min: the video watched at most twice, every exercise done until correct, the first-attempt score noted, anything under 80 percent redone the next day, 10 words to Anki per series.
- **1jour1actu pattern:** the three newest articles on 1jour1actu.com in one sitting, 30 min: each read once with its video where there is one, once without, then one sentence in French about it in the notebook. Look up at most 5 words per article; add 5 cards in all.
- **RFI reading pattern:** the next A2 exercise at francaisfacile.rfi.fr ("Exercices", filtered to A2), 30 min: the audio once blind, the transcript read with at most 10 lookups, the exercises done and checked, the transcript read aloud once. Add 5 cards. Note the title in the tracker.
- **Extra pattern:** one episode of *Extra en français* per week, in order from episode 3, 40 min: first viewing with French subtitles, second viewing of the same episode without. Write 5 phrases you would use yourself.
- **Easy French pattern:** two episodes of *Easy French* per step, 35 min (start with the *Super Easy French* playlist, move to the main episodes when those feel easy). The subtitles are burned in, so: first viewing audio only (screen face down), second viewing watching with the subtitles. Write the interviewer's question and three answers you understood, in French.
- **Un gars, une fille pattern:** one block of four short episodes (about 25 minutes of sketches) from the official *Un gars, une fille* channel on YouTube, in the order the channel lists them, 40 min: watched once with French auto-subtitles, once without. Write 5 spoken-register phrases (*chais pas, t'as vu, c'est ouf, bon ben, ça te dit ?*) and one sentence in French about the funniest sketch. Tracker.
- **Story pattern (SSF):** each of the eight stories in three intensive sittings of 25 minutes, chapter by chapter: listen to the audiobook chapter once blind, read the chapter looking up every unknown word not in the book's glossary, listen again with the text, answer the book's comprehension questions and check; 25 cards per story. Then one 60-minute step: the extensive pass (the whole story's audio at normal speed while reading along, no stopping, then once more without the text) and the retelling (a 100-word summary in French in the past tenses and a 90-second oral retelling without notes, Correction protocol on both).
- **Film pattern:** the film watched with French subtitles, extensive rules (no pausing, no dictionary); then a scene study: pick one 3-minute scene, watch it three more times, the last time with the subtitles covered, and write 5 lines of its dialogue from memory, checked against the subtitles. Then 5 sentences in French about the plot, Correction protocol. Tracker. 2 h.
- **Song pattern:** first write out the previous song's lyrics from memory and check them, accents included (not for the very first song). Then the new one: find the lyrics (search the title plus "paroles") and a recording, listen twice following the lyrics, look up every word you do not know, and sing along 3 times. From today this is the daily song: sing it twice through every day until the next song step replaces it. 20 min. Done when the previous song is written out with no lyric errors and the new one has been sung 3 times.
- **Monologue pattern:** one Recording protocol monologue of 2–3 minutes on the next topic of Appendix F (part 2), transcribed and corrected. 20 min.
- **Dictation pattern:** take 6–8 sentences (about 90 seconds) of audio with a transcript: an Assimil dialogue you have done, a chapter of the reader's audio, or a podcast segment. Play it in 5-second chunks and write every word. Compare with the transcript, count wrong or missing words (a wrong accent, a missing *-s* or *-e* of agreement, or a wrong silent ending such as *-er* for *-é* counts half), and record the percentage in `writing/dictations.md`. Rewrite each wrong word 3 times. Target at exit: 12 percent or less. 15 min.
- **Roleplay pattern:** write a 12-line dialogue between yourself and the other party in the situation named, Correction protocol, then record it playing *both* parts, changing your voice for the other speaker, without reading if you can. Do it twice. 25 min.

- [ ] **A2-017** [Study · Deep · 60 min] Grammar: the *passé composé* in depth.
      Do: read Appendix B point 1, COLL's chapter on the perfect tense and the GP débutant chapters on the *passé composé* (do and check the exercises). Two auxiliaries. *Avoir* for most verbs: *j'ai mangé, tu as fini, il a vendu*. *Être* for the verbs of movement and change of state, learned as a list: *aller, venir, arriver, partir, entrer, sortir, monter, descendre, naître, mourir, rester, tomber, retourner, passer, devenir, revenir, rentrer*; with *être* the participle agrees with the subject like an adjective: *elle est allée, ils sont partis, elles sont venues, nous sommes restés*. Six of them take *avoir* when they have a direct object and then change meaning: *J'ai sorti le chien. Elle a monté les valises. Il a passé une semaine à Nice. Nous avons descendu les cartons.* All reflexive and reciprocal verbs take *être*: *je me suis levé(e), elle s'est lavée, ils se sont rencontrés*; but no agreement when the direct object follows the verb (*elle s'est lavé les mains*) or when the pronoun is indirect (*ils se sont téléphoné, elles se sont écrit*). Past participles: *-er → -é*, *-ir → -i*, *-re → -u*, and the irregulars by heart: *eu, été, fait, pris, mis, dit, écrit, vu, lu, bu, su, pu, voulu, dû, venu, tenu, ouvert, offert, mort, né, connu, reçu, vécu, plu, ri, suivi, peint, craint*. - [ ] **A2-030** [Study · Deep · 60 min] Grammar: the *imparfait* and choosing between the pasts.
      Do: read Appendix B point 2, COLL's chapter on the imperfect and the GP débutant chapters on the *imparfait* and on *passé composé / imparfait*. Forms: take the *nous* form of the present, drop *-ons*, add *-ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient*: *nous finissons → je finissais; nous prenons → tu prenais; nous faisons → il faisait; nous buvons → nous buvions*. The only irregular stem is *être*: *j'étais, tu étais, il était, nous étions, vous étiez, ils étaient*. Spelling keeps the sound: *je mangeais, nous mangions; je commençais, nous commencions*. Four endings sound the same (*-ais, -ais, -ait, -aient*); you must write the right one. Uses: habits and repeated actions in the past (*Quand j'étais petit, je jouais dans la rue*), descriptions and background (*Il faisait froid et la maison était vide*), age and time in the past (*J'avais dix ans. Il était trois heures.*), an ongoing action interrupted by an event (*Je lisais quand le téléphone a sonné*), and politeness (*Je voulais vous demander ...*). The contrast: the *imparfait* paints the scene, the *passé composé* moves the story.       Do: read Appendix B point 5, COLL's chapter on the future and the GP débutant chapters on *le futur proche* and *le futur simple*. *Futur proche*: *aller* in the present + infinitive, for plans and what is about to happen: *Je vais partir. ## Appendix A: What changes at A2

1. **Input becomes the main course.** At A1 the textbook was the meal and input was dessert. At A2 you spend more hours listening and reading than studying. Expect to feel that you understand less than you "should"; that feeling is what growth sounds like. French in particular hides its words in speech (*je ne sais pas* is *chais pas*, *il y a* is *y a*, *tu as* is *t'as*), so the listening steps are deliberately heavier than the reading ones.
2. **Two podcasts, one pattern.** *Little Talk in Slow French* (one woman talking slowly about everyday France) and *InnerFrench* (one man talking a little faster about France and about learning, with free transcripts). Blind first, always. The transcript is a check, not a crutch.
3. **Audiobook plus text.** For *Short Stories in French*, always listen to a chapter before reading it, and always finish a story with a full-speed listen while reading. This is the fastest way to close the gap between your reading and your listening, which in French is wider than in any other language you are likely to have met.
4. **Assimil's second wave.** From Week 8 every day begins with an old Assimil lesson translated back into French. Translation into the language is the one exercise that shows you exactly what you cannot yet say. Keep the second wave going one lesson a day into B1 until lesson 100.
5. **Dictation.** About every other week from Week 4. It is the most honest listening test there is and it fixes the silent letters, the agreements and the accents as a side effect; at B1 it becomes the weekly *dictée* that French schoolchildren know. Log the percentages; watch them fall.
6. **Talking to a machine.** The AI voice sessions are for fluency, not accuracy. Do not stop to fix yourself. Get the error list afterwards. If a session goes badly, it still counts; a bad session is practice.
7. **Read whole books.** Eight graded stories, two readers, three *Petit Nicolas* stories and *Le Petit Prince* are the first French books you will finish. The first one is the hardest. Intensive on the first pass, extensive on the second, and always a summary afterwards. *Le Petit Prince* also teaches you to read the *passé simple*, which you will meet in every novel from now on and never need to say.
8. **Grammar is now about the past and about pronouns.** A1 was the present; A2 is telling what happened. The three things that matter most at this level are choosing between *passé composé* and *imparfait* (and getting the auxiliary and the agreement right), placing the object pronouns with *y* and *en* in their order, and the imperative. The checks look for them specifically. The subjunctive appears at the end only so that B1 does not start from zero.
9. **Songs continue** because they lodge whole phrases with the right rhythm and liaisons. Dassin, Piaf, Trenet, Brassens, Stromae and Angèle are the A2 songbook; a French speaker of any age can sing along, and so will you. Belgium and Québec are in it on purpose.
10. **Culture reading is still in English** where it has to be (the Republic, the Occupation, the BD), but the tracker now has French texts actually read in French: Apollinaire, Verlaine, Hugo, Prévert, La Fontaine, Goscinny, Saint-Exupéry. From B1 the culture track is in French only.

## Appendix B: A2 grammar checklist

1. **Passé composé in depth**: *avoir* and *être*; the seventeen *être* verbs; agreement with *être*; the six that take *avoir* with a direct object; reflexives with *être* and their agreement rules; the irregular participles; agreement with a preceding direct object recognised; the place of *ne ... pas* and of short adverbs.
2. **Imparfait and tense contrast**: the *nous* stem + *-ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient*; *j'étais*; the spelling of *mangeais, commençais*; background, habit, description, age and time, interrupted action against single events; the markers of each.
3. **Object pronouns**: direct *me, te, le, la, l', nous, vous, les*; indirect *me, te, lui, nous, vous, leur*; position before the verb, the infinitive and the auxiliary; the order *me/te/nous/vous > le/la/les > lui/leur*; which verbs take *à* + person.
4. ***Y* and *en***: *y* for *à* + thing or place; *en* for *de* + thing and for quantities, with the number kept after the verb; order after the other pronouns; the imperative forms *vas-y, prends-en*; the fixed phrases.
5. **The futures**: *aller* + infinitive; the *futur simple* endings on the infinitive; the eighteen irregular stems; *si* + present, future; *quand* + future.
6. **The imperative with pronouns**: *tu, nous, vous* forms; the dropped *-s*; *sois, aie, sache, veuillez*; pronouns after with hyphens and *moi / toi*; before in the negative; the infinitive as a written command.
7. **Relative pronouns**: *qui* subject, *que* object, *où* place and time, *dont* for *de*; *ce qui, ce que, ce dont*; *lequel* and *qui* after prepositions recognised; the relative never dropped.
8. **Comparison**: *plus / moins / aussi ... que*; *plus de / moins de / autant de ... que*; *meilleur, mieux, pire*; the superlative with its repeated article and *de*; *de plus en plus*.
9. **Negation**: *ne ... plus, jamais, rien, personne, que, aucun, nulle part, ni ... ni*; their place in the *passé composé* and before an infinitive; *personne* and *rien* as subjects; short answers; the spoken drop of *ne*, recognised and never written.
10. **Time and duration**: *depuis* + present; *ça fait ... que, il y a ... que*; *il y a* = ago; *pendant, pour, en, dans, jusqu'à, à partir de, au bout de, vers*; *depuis* with a negative and the *passé composé*.
11. **Conditionnel présent**: the future stem + *imparfait* endings; politeness, wish, advice; *si* + *imparfait*, conditional; the future in the past recognised.
12. **Adverbs and *tout***: *-ment* from the feminine, *-amment / -emment*; *bien, mal, mieux, vite*; position; *tout* as adjective (agrees) and as adverb (*toute seule* only before a feminine consonant).
13. **Pronominal verbs**: reflexive, reciprocal, idiomatic; the pronoun matching the subject before an infinitive; *être* in the *passé composé*; negation and imperative.
14. **The gerund**: *en* + *-ant*, same subject as the main verb; "while" and "by"; *tout en*; *étant, ayant, sachant*.
15. **Plus-que-parfait and the narrative tenses**: *avais / étais* + participle; sequence of tenses in a story; reported speech shifting back recognised; the *passé simple* read on sight for the twelve commonest verbs and the three regular endings.
16. **Indefinites**: *quelqu'un / personne, quelque chose / rien, quelque part / nulle part, tout, chacun, chaque, plusieurs, certains, quelques-uns, aucun, n'importe qui / quoi / où, l'un ... l'autre, la plupart*; *de* + masculine adjective after them; *on*.
17. **The passive, recognised**: *être* + agreeing participle, *par*; *on* and the pronominal verb preferred in speech.
18. **Sixty irregular verbs**, with present *je / nous / ils*, participle, future stem and *passé simple* *il*: *être, avoir, aller, faire, pouvoir, vouloir, devoir, savoir, venir, tenir, prendre, mettre, dire, lire, écrire, voir, boire, croire, connaître, naître, mourir, vivre, suivre, partir, sortir, dormir, servir, sentir, ouvrir, offrir, souffrir, découvrir, courir, recevoir, apercevoir, falloir, pleuvoir, valoir, envoyer, payer, essayer, appeler, jeter, acheter, lever, préférer, espérer, manger, commencer, conduire, construire, plaire, rire, battre, vaincre, craindre, peindre, joindre, résoudre, s'asseoir*.
19. **Present subjunctive, first frames**: the *ils* stem + *-e, -es, -e, -ions, -iez, -ent*; *sois, aie, aille, fasse, puisse, sache, veuille, faille, pleuve*; used after *il faut que, je veux / je voudrais que, pour que*; recognised after *il est important / possible que, je ne pense pas que, bien que, avant que, j'ai peur que*; the infinitive when the subject is the same.
20. **Reported speech in the present**: *dire que*; *demander si*; question words kept; *ce que / ce qui* for *qu'est-ce que / qui*; *dire de* + infinitive for commands; pronoun, possessive and time shifts; the past frame recognised.

## Appendix E: A2 exit reading test

**Un week-end à Lyon**

Le mois dernier, Camille et son copain Thomas ont passé un week-end à Lyon. Ils n'y étaient jamais allés, mais une collègue de Camille leur avait dit que c'était la ville où on mangeait le mieux en France. Ils ont pris le train à Paris le vendredi soir ; le voyage a duré deux heures. Quand ils sont arrivés, il faisait nuit et il pleuvait un peu.

Ils avaient réservé une petite chambre dans le Vieux-Lyon, le quartier de la Renaissance, avec ses rues étroites et ses passages secrets qu'on appelle les traboules. Depuis la fenêtre, on voyait la basilique de Fourvière, tout en haut de la colline. Le samedi matin, ils se sont levés tôt et ils sont montés à Fourvière à pied, parce que Thomas ne voulait pas prendre le funiculaire. Camille, elle, préférait flâner dans les rues et regarder les vitrines. Ils se sont disputés un peu, puis ils ont ri.

À midi, ils ont mangé dans un bouchon, un petit restaurant typique de Lyon. Thomas a commandé une quenelle de brochet et Camille a pris une salade lyonnaise ; ils n'avaient jamais goûté ces plats. Le serveur leur a expliqué que la cuisine lyonnaise était plus lourde que la cuisine parisienne, et qu'il fallait marcher beaucoup après le repas. Ils ont suivi son conseil et ils se sont promenés le long de la Saône jusqu'au soir.

Le dimanche, il a plu toute la journée, ce qui est rare en juin. Ils sont donc allés au musée des Confluences, à la pointe de la presqu'île, entre les deux fleuves. Là, ils ont rencontré un couple de Québécois qui visitait la France pour la première fois. Ces derniers leur ont recommandé de revenir en décembre pour la Fête des lumières, quand toute la ville est éclairée pendant quatre nuits.

Dans le train du retour, Thomas a dit que c'était le meilleur week-end de l'année. Camille était d'accord, mais elle avait un peu mal aux pieds. « La prochaine fois, on prend le funiculaire », a-t-elle dit, et elle a souri.

Questions (répondez en français, avec des phrases complètes) :

1. Quand Camille et Thomas sont-ils allés à Lyon ?
2. Étaient-ils déjà allés à Lyon avant ?
3. Qu'est-ce que la collègue de Camille leur avait dit sur Lyon ?
4. Comment sont-ils allés à Lyon et combien de temps a duré le voyage ?
5. Qu'est-ce qu'on voyait depuis la fenêtre de leur chambre ?
6. Pourquoi sont-ils montés à Fourvière à pied ?
7. Qu'est-ce que Thomas a commandé au restaurant ?
8. Qu'est-ce que le serveur leur a conseillé de faire après le repas ?
9. Qui leur a recommandé de revenir en décembre, et pourquoi ?
10. Pourquoi Camille veut-elle prendre le funiculaire la prochaine fois ?

**Key:** 1. Le mois dernier (en juin), pour un week-end. 2. Non, ils n'y étaient jamais allés. 3. Que c'était la ville où on mangeait le mieux en France. 4. En train, depuis Paris ; le voyage a duré deux heures. 5. La basilique de Fourvière, en haut de la colline. 6. Parce que Thomas ne voulait pas prendre le funiculaire. 7. Une quenelle de brochet. 8. De marcher beaucoup (de se promener). 9. Un couple de Québécois, pour la Fête des lumières, quand toute la ville est éclairée. 10. Parce qu'elle avait mal aux pieds.

## Appendix J: Resource acquisition notes

- **Assimil *French* / *New French with Ease*** (Anthony Bulger): the current edition in the *With Ease* series is sold as *French* (book alone, or the pack with the MP3 audio; about 800 pages, 100 lessons, aiming at B2); the older printing, *New French with Ease*, has 113 lessons and four CDs and is still common second-hand. Either works; the lesson numbers in this file assume 100, and the two editions agree closely for the first 100. assimil.com ships worldwide, and Amazon carries it. The book alone is useless without the audio. The method's two phases are the passive first wave (lessons 1–100) and the active second wave (from lesson 50, translating lesson 1 onward back into French); this guide follows that design.
- **Short Stories in French for Beginners**, Olly Richards, Teach Yourself, 2018; 8 stories with glossaries and comprehension questions; audiobook on Audible and in the Teach Yourself Library app. Mapped to A2–B1. A second volume exists for the failure-handling reread if you want fresh material.
- **Graded readers**: *Lectures CLE en français facile* (CLE International) is the largest French graded series, with four levels; niveau 2 is A2 and its titles include *Arsène Lupin: L'Aiguille creuse*, *Les Misérables*, *Deux ans de vacances*, *Paul et Virginie* and *Le Roman de la momie*, sold with a CD or with the audio as a download (check the current catalogue at cle-international.com; levels and audio formats have shifted between printings, and the 6-in-10 test from the A1 notes settles any doubt). Hachette FLE's *Lire en français facile* is the equivalent series at A2 and is the fallback. Buy from the publisher, from a French bookshop online (Decitre, Fnac, Librairie Eyrolles) or from Amazon.
- ***Mondes en VF*** (Didier): short original novels by contemporary Francophone writers graded A1 to B1, each with a free MP3 reading and a teaching file at mondesenvf.fr; the A2 titles include *La Cravate de Simenon* (Nicolas Ancion) and *Nico et le village maudit*. They are on the free-reading list rather than in the steps.
- ***Le Petit Nicolas***, Goscinny and Sempé, 1960: the first collection (IMAV éditions or the Gallimard Folio edition) has nineteen stories; *Un souvenir qu'on va chérir*, *Les cow-boys* and *Le Bouillon* are the first three. The audiobook is on Audible and on the streaming services; readings of single stories are on YouTube. The remaining stories and the later collections are B1 free reading.
- ***Le Petit Prince***, Saint-Exupéry, 1943: Gallimard Folio or the illustrated Gallimard edition, any bookshop. In France the text stays in copyright until 2032, so it is not on litteratureaudio.com; it is public domain in Canada and most other countries, where fr.wikisource.org carries it. Full readings are on YouTube (search the title plus "livre audio complet"); Gérard Philipe's 1954 recording is abridged and the Audible reading by Bernard Giraudeau is complete. The 2015 film is on the usual rental platforms and often on Netflix.
- **Canon texts online**: fr.wikisource.org has Apollinaire's *Alcools*, Verlaine's *Poèmes saturniens* and *Romances sans paroles*, Hugo's *Les Contemplations* and La Fontaine's *Fables* in full; litteratureaudio.com has free readings of all of them. Prévert's *Paroles* (1946) is in copyright: buy the Folio paperback, which you will keep through C2; Montand's spoken *Barbara* and sung *Les Feuilles mortes* are on YouTube and the streaming services.
- ***Grammaire progressive du français*** (CLE International): *niveau débutant* as at A1; *niveau intermédiaire* (the current edition, with its separate *corrigés* booklet or the version with the answers) is the one for Week 9 onward and all of B1; buy both the book and the *corrigés*. **Collins *Easy Learning French Grammar***: as at A1; read the named chapters and use the index. ***Bescherelle: La conjugaison pour tous*** (Hatier): as at A1; the free conjugator at bescherelle.com or Reverso does the same job on a phone.
- **TV5Monde *Apprendre le français***: apprendre.tv5monde.com, free with an account; the A2 collection is organised in series (each built on a short authentic video with four to six exercises). "Two series" in this file means the next two in the order the site lists them; if a series is long, the next two exercises count as one. If the A2 list runs out before Week 15, continue with the first B1 series.
- ***1jour1actu***: 1jour1actu.com, free, from the children's weekly of Milan Presse; one article a day on real news in plain French for 8- to 12-year-olds, many with a one-minute *1jour1question* video. The site also has a free weekly PDF of the paper if you prefer to print.
- ***Le français facile avec RFI***: francaisfacile.rfi.fr, free; the "Exercices" section is filtered by level and each exercise is a short real radio extract with its full transcript and self-checking questions. The daily *Journal en français facile* on the same site is the B1 daily; do not start it yet.
- ***Little Talk in Slow French***: Nagisa Morimoto's podcast, free in every podcast app, about 20 minutes an episode, slow and clear; the transcripts are published on the podcast's Patreon, mostly behind a small monthly subscription, which is the price of the pattern's reading stage. The fallback, ***Coffee Break French*** season 2 (Radio Lingua), is free with its audio, is taught in English with French dialogues, and sells its transcripts in the Coffee Break Academy; its French dialogues are short enough to transcribe yourself and check with the AI if you prefer not to pay.
- ***InnerFrench***: Hugo Cotton's podcast, free, with a free transcript for every episode at innerfrench.com; start from episode 1 and go in order. His YouTube channel is part of the comprehensible-input daily from Week 10.
- **Commute listening** (A2-010): the ***Duolingo French Podcast*** is free in every podcast app, with transcripts at podcast.duolingo.com; it is narrated in English with the stories told in slow, clear French by speakers from France, Québec, Belgium, Switzerland and Africa, and it is the only thing in the commute slot at A2, in order from its first episode. *Coffee Break French* and *Français authentique* are good extras for free time, outside the plan.
- ***Extra en français***: 13 episodes (2002), widely uploaded to YouTube in full, several playlists with French subtitles. Episodes 1–2 were done at A1.
- ***Easy French***: youtube.com/@EasyFrench; street interviews in Paris and elsewhere with burned-in French and English subtitles; new episodes weekly. The *Super Easy French* playlist is the entry point.
- ***Un gars, une fille***: Canal+, 1999–2003, about 480 six-minute episodes; the official *Un gars, une fille* YouTube channel carries them free, in blocks and as single episodes, with auto-subtitles. The Québec original (Radio-Canada, 1997–2003, Guy A. Lepage and Sylvie Léonard) is on ICI Tou.tv in Canada and partly on YouTube.
- **The comprehensible-input video**: Alice Ayel's channel and the *French Comprehensible Input* channel as at A1, moving up through their levels; from Week 10 add *Easy French* episodes not used as steps and the *InnerFrench* YouTube channel. Keep a rough tally of the hours (YouTube's history or a line in the notebook).
- **Films**: *Kirikou et la Sorcière* (1998), *Ernest et Célestine* (2012), *Intouchables* (2011), *Bienvenue chez les Ch'tis* (2008), *Le Dîner de cons* (1998) and *Le Petit Prince* (2015) are all on the usual rental platforms (Apple, Amazon, Google) and periodically on Netflix, Prime or Canal+, with French subtitles; a French DVD or Blu-ray always has them. Outside France, Amazon's French store and a region-free player are the fallback.
- **Music**: all songs in this file are on Spotify, Apple Music and YouTube; lyrics on Genius, Paroles.net or by searching the title plus "paroles". Brel's *Olympia 1964* is streaming everywhere.
- ***A Frequency Dictionary of French: Core Vocabulary for Learners***, Deryle Lonsdale and Yvan Le Bras, Routledge, 2009, ISBN 9780415775311 (paperback): 5000 words in frequency order from a 23-million-word corpus, each with an example sentence and translation, plus thematic lists. The free alternative is any 2000-word French frequency shared deck on AnkiWeb, used in Anki's browser as a list and not merged into `Français`.
- **AI voice conversation**: ChatGPT Voice, Langua (languatalk.com) and TalkPal all support French well, with a choice of France or Québec voices in the dedicated apps. Any of them works for the habit; what matters is 10 minutes every study day, in French only.
- **BonPatron** (bonpatron.com): a free French grammar and spelling checker that explains each flag; use it on the journal before the Sunday correction, never instead of it.
- **Official samples**: France Éducation international publishes DELF A2 *exemples de sujets* (*tout public*; the four papers, the audio and the *corrigés*) free at france-education-international.fr under DELF-DALF, and delfdalf.fr mirrors them. The exam is marked out of 100, 25 per skill, pass mark 50 with at least 5 in each skill.
