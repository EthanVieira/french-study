# Level A1: Breakthrough

**Goal at exit:** you can introduce yourself, describe your family, home, job and daily routine, handle shopping, directions, ordering and simple small talk, understand slow clear French on familiar topics, read simple texts, and write short simple messages. You know roughly 800 to 1000 words, the core of French grammar (gender and articles, agreement, the present tense of the regular groups and the main irregular verbs, *c'est* vs *il est*, the partitive, negation, questions, one past tense), you can read any French word aloud from its spelling, and you pronounce French well enough (the nasal vowels, *u* against *ou*, the *r*, liaison, the final-syllable rhythm) that a native speaker understands you first time.

**Time:** the dailies take 1 to 1.5 hours a day at this level and the steps add up to about 105 hours: about 200 hours in all. At the planned pace, over 12 weeks, that is roughly an hour and a quarter of steps a day, 6 days a week. If you only have time for the dailies and one short step a day, keep the order and let the level take 20 weeks.

**Prerequisite:** read [README.md](README.md) fully, especially the five standard methods. The step text below refers to them by name (LRS cycle, Anki rules, Recording protocol, Correction protocol, Intensive/Extensive).

## The A1 day

Every study day has the same shape: **the dailies, in the order of the table below, and then the current step**, which is the first unticked step in this file. A step is one sitting with one focus. If you finish it with time left, start the next; if it is too long for today, stop at a natural break and resume it tomorrow. Nothing else needs scheduling. There are no weekly lanes to juggle, and the week headings are only pacing marks of about six study days.

| Daily | Minutes | Starts at | Ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anki: all due cards, then the new ones | 15 | A1-004 | never |
| Pronunciation drill: the *r*, the nasal vowels, *u/ou* | 10 | A1-020 | at the sound check, A1-033 |
| Commute slot: Language Transfer, the next two tracks | 25 | A1-026 | at track 40 |
| Commute slot after Language Transfer: the next *Coffee Break French* season 1 lesson | 25 | A1-090 | A2 changes its content |
| The daily song, sung twice | 5 | A1-040 | never (the daily poem or song from C1) |
| *Le journal*, 3 to 5 sentences | 10 | A1-077 | end of B2 (the *journal de lecture* from C1) |
| Comprehensible-input video (Alice Ayel, then also *French Comprehensible Input*) | 15, then 20, then 30 | A1-027 | end of A2 |
| One children's television episode | 10 | A1-043 | end of A1 |

A `Habit` step starts, changes or ends a daily: tick it on the first day you do the daily. Everything that is not in this table is a step, and steps are done strictly in order.

Five things repeat every week from Block 2 on. They are defined here once; the weekly steps that name them are one-liners.

- **The unit pattern** (CF units 1–8, four steps each, always the same): **(a) Dialogues**: LRS cycle on every dialogue and listening passage in the unit; done when all six LRS stages are done for every dialogue and the recording is saved. **(b) Grammar and exercises**: read the unit's language notes; copy each rule into the notebook with the book's example plus one example of your own; do every exercise in the unit in writing; check against the key; redo every wrong item and write in the notebook *why* it was wrong; done when all exercises are done, checked, and wrong ones redone with reasons written. **(c) Vocabulary and shadowing**: every new word in the unit's vocabulary list goes into Anki per the Anki rules, with gender and the plural when irregular (usually 25–40 cards; spread new cards over a few days if needed), then "shadow live" all dialogues 3 more times and record one of them; done when all unit vocabulary is in Anki, 3 live shadows done, one recorded. **(d) Production**: write 8 sentences about your own life using this unit's grammar and words; Correction protocol; copy the corrected sentences by hand into the notebook; done when the corrected sentences are in the notebook.
- **The TV5Monde rule** (from A1-038): open apprendre.tv5monde.com, choose niveau A1, and take the next exercise series in the order the site lists them (a series is one short authentic video with four to six self-checking exercises on listening, vocabulary and grammar). Watch the video at most twice per exercise and do every exercise until the site marks it correct. Note the series' title and your first-attempt score in the notebook. Any exercise under 80 percent on the first attempt is redone the next day. Add 10 words from the series to Anki. Done when the series is completed, its score noted, any exercise under 80 percent redone, 10 cards added.
- **The song pattern** (from A1-041): first write out the previous song's lyrics from memory and check them, accents included (not for the very first song). Then the new one: find the lyrics (search the title plus "paroles") and a recording, listen twice following the lyrics, look up every word you do not know, and sing along 3 times. From today this is the daily song: sing it twice through every day until the next song step replaces it. Done when the previous song is written out from memory with no lyric errors and the new one has been sung 3 times.
- **The monologue pattern** (from A1-055): Recording protocol. Speak for 60 to 90 seconds on the topic the step names, without notes beyond 5 keywords. Transcribe, Correction protocol, copy the corrections into the notebook. Done when the recording is saved and the corrections are copied.
- **The dictée pattern** (from A1-056): choose 4 sentences from the current unit's audio (different dialogues, 8 to 15 words each). Play each sentence 3 times, writing it down; do not look at the book. Then check against the book's transcript: a missing or wrong accent, a wrong agreement, a missing silent letter (*-s*, *-e*, *-ent*) or a wrong homophone (*a/à, ou/où, et/est, ces/ses, son/sont*) is an error. Copy every sentence that had an error 3 times by hand and log the error types in the notebook; an error type that appears in 3 dictées becomes an Anki card. Done when 4 sentences are written, checked and logged.

## Block 1: Sounds, reading rules, alphabet, numbers (Week 1, days 3–7, and Week 2)

French spelling is historical and French sound has what English lacks, so this block does two things before any grammar: it teaches you to read any word aloud from its spelling (a closed set of rules that holds for life), and it sets the sounds English speakers get wrong (the four nasal vowels, *u* against *ou*, the uvular *r*, the mute *e*, liaison, the even final-stressed rhythm). All of it is retested at every milestone.

- [ ] **A1-015** [Study · Deep · 45 min, one sitting] The alphabet and spelling aloud.
      Do: French uses the same 26 letters as English, but their names are different and you will need them for spelling your name, your email and your street over the phone: *a, bé, cé, dé, e (the mute e sound), èf, gé, ache, i, ji, ka, èl, èm, èn, o, pé, ku, èr, èss, té, u (the French u), vé, double vé, iks, i grec, zèd*. The accents are named when spelling: *e accent aigu* (é), *e accent grave* (è), *e accent circonflexe* (ê), *e tréma* (ë), *c cédille* (ç); a double letter is *deux* (*deux l*); *@* is *arobase*, *.* is *point*, *-* is *tiret*. On Larousse or Forvo listen to each letter name 3 times, repeating aloud, then search YouTube for "chanson de l'alphabet français" (any children's alphabet song) and sing along 5 times. Write the alphabet by hand 5 times, saying each letter name aloud. Then spell aloud, letter by letter and with accent names: *Paris, Marseille, Genève, Bruxelles, Montréal, Québec, Dakar, Françoise, Hélène, Jérôme*.
      Done when: you can say all 26 letter names in order from memory in under 30 seconds, and can spell your own name, your email address and the ten names aloud with French letter and accent names.

- [ ] **A1-016** [Study · Deep · 50 min] Reading rules 1: silent letters, *-ent*, the mute *e*, the accents.
      Do: this step and the next give you the rules for reading a French word aloud from its spelling. Watch one YouTube video on French silent letters (search "French silent letters final consonants"), then write the rules below with all their example words in the notebook, look up every word on Larousse, play it, and say each 5 times.
      1. Most final consonants are silent: *petit, grand, trop, nez, chez, deux, trois, vous, nous, blanc, long, temps, froid, tard, lit, prix*. The consonants that are usually pronounced at the end are *c, r, f, l* (remember "careful"): *sac, parc, avec, lac · pour, mer, car, hiver · chef, neuf, œuf, actif · mal, hôtel, sel, avril*. Exceptions you must know: *-er* at the end of a verb or a long word is silent (*parler, manger, boulanger, premier*, said "-é"), and *tabac, blanc, estomac, porc, clef* have silent *c* or *f*.
      2. A final *-e* is silent, but it makes the consonant before it sound: *petit / petite, grand / grande, français / française, vert / verte, blond / blonde, étudiant / étudiante*. This is how French marks the feminine in speech.
      3. *-ent* at the end of a verb is completely silent: *ils parlent* sounds exactly like *il parle*; *ils mangent, elles habitent, ils aiment*. In any other word *-ent* is the nasal vowel: *souvent, un moment, lentement, un enfant, le vent*.
      4. Plural *-s* and *-x* are silent: *les livres* sounds like *le livre* apart from the article; *les amis, des animaux, les yeux, deux châteaux*.
      5. *h* is always silent: *homme, hôtel, habiter, heure, hiver* (whether or not it allows liaison is a separate point, A1-021).
      6. The accents. *é* (accent aigu) is a closed "ay" with no glide: *été, café, télé, école, parlé*. *è* and *ê* (grave, circonflexe) are an open "eh" as in "bed": *mère, père, très, frère, fête, tête, être, forêt*. *à* and *ù* only distinguish words and do not change the sound: *a / à, ou / où, la / là*. *â, î, ô, û* mostly mark a lost *s* (*hôpital* = hospital, *forêt* = forest, *île* = isle, *août*, *sûr*) and barely change the sound. *ç* (cédille) makes *c* sound as *s* before *a, o, u*: *ça, garçon, français, leçon, reçu*. *ë* and *ï* (tréma) separate two vowels that would otherwise merge: *Noël, naïf, maïs, Haïti*.
      Done when: the 6 rules and all their example words are in the notebook, and you have heard and said each word.

- [ ] **A1-017** [Study · Deep · 50 min] Reading rules 2: the vowel and consonant spellings.
      Do: same procedure (look up, play, say 5 times, write in the notebook) for the spellings that do not sound as an English speaker expects.
      1. *eau, au* and *o* are all one closed "o": *eau, beau, chapeau, bateau, gâteau, autre, haut, jaune, mot, vélo, photo*.
      2. *ai* and *ei* are the open "eh" of *è*: *mais, j'ai, lait, maison, français, neige, seize, treize, beige*. Final *-er, -ez, -é, -et* are the closed "ay" of *é*: *parler, chez, allez, nez, été, et, billet*.
      3. *ou* is the "oo" of "food": *vous, nous, jour, tout, rouge, bonjour, douze*. *u* is the French *u* that English does not have (A1-019): *tu, rue, lune, une, sur, bus, salut*.
      4. *oi* is "wa": *moi, toi, trois, voiture, froid, bonsoir, noir, poisson, voilà*. *oin* is "wan" with a nasal: *loin, point, moins*.
      5. *eu* and *œu* are rounded "uh" sounds: *deux, peu, bleu, jeudi, heureux* (closed) and *fleur, sœur, cœur, œuf, neuf, peur* (open).
      6. *gn* is the "ny" of "canyon": *montagne, campagne, gagner, espagnol, champagne, Espagne*.
      7. *ch* is "sh": *chat, chien, chambre, chercher, chocolat, Michel, dimanche*. (*ph* = f: *photo, pharmacie*; *th* = t: *thé, théâtre*.)
      8. *ill* is "y": *fille, famille, gentille, travailler, billet, juillet*; *-ail, -eil, -euil, -ouil* end in that "y": *travail, soleil, réveil, fauteuil, œil*. The exceptions, with a real *l*: *ville, mille, tranquille, village*.
      9. *c* is "s" before *e, i, y* and "k" elsewhere: *ce, ici, cinéma, merci / car, cou, école, sac*. *g* is the "zh" of "measure" before *e, i, y* and hard "g" elsewhere: *gens, girafe, manger, Gérard / gare, gros, garçon, bague*. *qu* is "k": *qui, que, quatre, quand, musique*. *gu* before *e, i* is hard "g": *guerre, guitare, langue*.
      10. A single *s* between vowels is "z"; *ss* is "s": *maison, chose, cousin, visage / poisson, aussi, classe, boisson*. *poison / poisson* and *désert / dessert* are different words.
      11. *y* is "i" and between vowels is two "i"s: *style, Yves, pays (pè-i), payer, voyage*. *ay, oy, uy* follow: *crayon, voyons*.
      12. *an, en, in, on, un* followed by a consonant or at the end of a word are nasal vowels, the subject of A1-018: *dans, temps, vin, bon, un*.
      Done when: the 12 rules and all their example words are in the notebook and you have heard and said each. Self-test: read aloud, without the dictionary, then check on Larousse: *beaucoup, maintenant, aujourd'hui, monsieur, femme, fils, œil, oignon, second, ville, fille, pays, gentil, tranquille, poisson, poison, chez, nez, jeune, jaune*. 17 of 20 right, or reread the rules and retest with 20 words from the CF Unit 1 glossary.

- [ ] **A1-018** [Study · Deep · 45 min] The four nasal vowels.
      Do: French has four vowels said through the nose, and English has none; they distinguish words (*vent / vin / vont*). Watch one YouTube video on the French nasal vowels (search "French nasal vowels an in on un"). The rule: a vowel plus *n* or *m* is nasal when the *n/m* ends the syllable (followed by a consonant or the end of the word); the *n/m* itself is not pronounced. Before a vowel or a double *nn/mm* there is no nasal: *bon* (nasal) but *bonne* (no nasal, real *n*); *an / année, vin / vinaigre, un / une, plein / pleine, américain / américaine*. The four:
      1. /ɑ̃/, written *an, am, en, em*: *dans, enfant, chambre, temps, cent, grand, vent, lent, blanc, France, décembre, comment*.
      2. /ɛ̃/, written *in, im, ain, aim, ein, yn* and *-ien*: *vin, cinq, pain, faim, plein, main, train, matin, simple, bien, rien, chien, vingt*.
      3. /ɔ̃/, written *on, om*: *bon, non, mon, nom, maison, pont, long, blond, bonjour, onze, nombre*.
      4. /œ̃/, written *un, um*: *un, brun, parfum, lundi, chacun*. In Paris today this one has mostly merged with /ɛ̃/ (*brin* and *brun* sound alike); you may say it that way, but keep *un* distinct from *on*.
      Write the four in the notebook with their spellings and 8 words each. Then the minimal sets, played on Larousse or Forvo and said 5 times each: *vent / vin / vont · banc / bain / bon · sans / saint / son · lent / lin / long · an / un / on · blanc / blond · temps / teint / ton · bon / bonne · an / année · plein / pleine*.
      Done when: the table and the sets are in the notebook; record the sets and play each next to the dictionary audio; you judge at most 2 of the 10 sets wrong, and *bon / bonne* and *plein / pleine* are clearly different.

- [ ] **A1-019** [Study · Deep · 45 min] *u* against *ou*, and the uvular *r*.
      Do: two sounds, two habits to build. (1) *u* (written *u*): say "ee" and, keeping the tongue exactly where it is, round the lips as for "oo". That is *tu*. *ou* (written *ou*) is the English "oo". They distinguish words constantly: *tu / tout, rue / roue, pur / pour, su / sous, lu / loup, vu / vous, bu / bout, dessus / dessous, du / doux, mur / mou*. Play each pair on Larousse or Forvo and say it 10 times. (2) The French *r* is made at the back of the mouth, where you gargle or clear your throat, with the tongue tip down behind the lower teeth; it is never the English *r* and never a Spanish trill. Watch one "how to pronounce the French r" video (search that phrase; there are several good ones). Start from a soft gargle on *ga-ra*, then: between vowels *Paris, merci, Marie, arriver*; at the end *pour, mer, bonjour, sur, noir*; at the start *rue, rouge, rien, restaurant, Robert*; in clusters, the hardest, *très, trois, quatre, arbre, frère, grand, livre, prendre*. A weak or breathy *r* is fine; a tongue-tip *r* is not.
      Québec option: Québec French kept the older tongue-tip *r* in places and its *u* and *ou* are the same as in France; learn the uvular *r* anyway, it is what Radio-Canada uses.
      Done when: the 10 pairs and the 20 *r* words are in the notebook; record them and compare with the dictionary audio; *tu / tout* and the other pairs are clearly different, and no *r* is an English *r*.

- [ ] **A1-021** [Study · Deep · 45 min] The mute *e*, liaison and elision.
      Do: three rules that make spoken French sound like one long word, and that are drilled in every LRS cycle from now on. Read the matching short section of a pronunciation guide (the CF introduction has one; so does any YouTube "French liaison and elision" video). Write the rules and examples in the notebook, play and say each example 5 times.
      1. **The mute *e***. The *e* without an accent in *je, me, te, se, le, de, ce, ne, que* and inside words like *petit, samedi, maintenant, boulangerie* is a weak "uh" that often disappears in speech: *je ne sais pas* is said *chais pas*, *petit* is *p'tit*, *samedi* is *sam'di*, *tout de suite* is *toud'suite*. At A1, say the *e* in careful speech and drop it only in these common cases; learn to *hear* it dropped everywhere.
      2. **Liaison**. A silent final consonant is pronounced when the next word starts with a vowel or a mute *h*, and it joins the next word: *les amis* (lé-za-mi), *nous avons* (nou-za-von), *vous êtes* (vou-zèt), *ils ont* (il-zon), *un homme* (un-nom), *un petit enfant* (p'ti-tan-fan), *deux heures* (deu-zeur), *trois ans*, *six ans*, *dix euros*, *mon ami*, *c'est un* (cè-tun), *est-il*, *grand ami* (gran-ta-mi, the *d* sounds as *t*), *en avril*. Liaison is compulsory after *les, des, mes, tes, ses, nos, vos, leurs, un, deux, trois, six, dix*, after the pronouns *nous, vous, ils, elles, on*, between an adjective and the noun after it, and in *c'est, est-ce, il est*. There is no liaison after *et* (*et il*, no *t*), before the numbers *un, huit, onze* (*les huit*), or before an *h aspiré* (*les héros, les haricots, le hibou*, marked with an asterisk in Larousse). *ils sont* (il-son) and *ils ont* (il-zon) are different words only by the liaison.
      3. **Elision**. *le, la, je, me, te, se, ne, de, que, ce* drop their vowel and take an apostrophe before a vowel or mute *h*: *l'ami, l'école, l'homme, l'heure, j'ai, j'habite, m'appelle, n'est pas, d'accord, qu'il, c'est*; *si* only before *il(s)*: *s'il vous plaît*. Never before an *h aspiré*: *le hibou, la honte*. A missing elision (*je ai*, *le ami*) is a real error, in speech and in writing.
      Done when: rules and 40 examples in the notebook; read aloud and record *Les amis de mes enfants ont deux heures. Vous êtes à l'hôtel ? C'est un homme. Ils sont ici et ils ont faim. J'habite à Paris et je n'ai pas de voiture.* and every liaison and elision is there and no false one is added.

- [ ] **A1-022** [Study · Deep · 40 min] Final-syllable stress and the even rhythm.
      Do: French has no word stress of the English kind. Every syllable gets the same length and clarity (no vowel is reduced to "uh"), and the only stress is a slight lengthening of the **last** syllable of a word group. *Paris* is *pa-RI*, *madame* is *ma-DAME*, *téléphone* is *té-lé-PHONE*, *université* is *u-ni-ver-si-TÉ*, and in a phrase only the last syllable of the whole group is stressed: *Je vais à Pa-RIS. Elle habite à Mar-SEILLE. Un café, s'il vous PLAÎT.* The English habit to kill is stressing the first syllable (*TEL-ephone, PAR-is, RES-taurant*): it is the single thing that makes an English accent instantly recognisable. - [ ] **A1-025** [Study · Deep · 45 min] Numbers 70–1000: the *soixante-dix* system, and *septante / nonante*.
      Do: France counts by twenties above 69. 70 is *soixante-dix* (60 + 10), then *soixante et onze* (71), *soixante-douze* (72) ... *soixante-dix-neuf* (79). 80 is *quatre-vingts* (4 × 20, with an *s*), then *quatre-vingt-un* (81, no *et*, no *s*), *quatre-vingt-deux* ... *quatre-vingt-neuf* (89). 90 is *quatre-vingt-dix* (4 × 20 + 10), then *quatre-vingt-onze* (91), *quatre-vingt-douze* ... *quatre-vingt-dix-neuf* (99). 100 is *cent*, 101 *cent un*, 110 *cent dix*, 200 *deux cents* (with *s*), 201 *deux cent un* (the *s* drops when another number follows), 350 *trois cent cinquante*, 999 *neuf cent quatre-vingt-dix-neuf*; 1000 is *mille* and never takes an *s* (*deux mille*). - [ ] **A1-052** [Study · Deep · 50 min] Subject pronouns, *tu* and *vous*, and *être, avoir, aller, faire*.
      Do: whether or not CF has covered them all yet, learn now by heart. Subject pronouns: *je (j'), tu, il, elle, on, nous, vous, ils, elles*. Rules: (1) French never drops the pronoun: *je parle*, not *parle*; (2) *on* is the everyday spoken "we" (*On va au cinéma ?*) and also "people, one"; it takes the *il* form of the verb; (3) *tu* is for family, friends, children, fellow students and anyone who says *tu* to you; *vous* is for strangers, shopkeepers, officials, anyone older or senior, and for any group of people; when in doubt use *vous* and let the other person propose *tu* (*On peut se tutoyer*); (4) *ils* for any mixed or masculine group, *elles* only for an all-feminine group. *Québec option*: *tu* is used more readily between adults of similar age; *vous* still for strangers and officials. The four verbs you cannot speak without: *être* (to be): *je suis, tu es, il est, nous sommes, vous êtes, ils sont*; *avoir* (to have): *j'ai, tu as, il a, nous avons, vous avez, ils ont*; *aller* (to go): *je vais, tu vas, il va, nous allons, vous allez, ils vont*; *faire* (to do, make): *je fais, tu fais, il fait, nous faisons* (said "fe-zon"), *vous faites, ils font*. ## Appendix A: How French works (primer)

1. **Family.** French is a Romance language, a daughter of the Latin spoken in Gaul and a sibling of Spanish, Italian, Portuguese and Catalan, shaped by Frankish (Germanic) in its sound and by a thousand years of Paris. Because English took about a third of its vocabulary from French after 1066, thousands of French words are recognisable on sight: *information, université, important, naturel, animal, hôpital, problème, gouvernement*. Learn the regular correspondences (*-tion* is the same, *-ty → -té*, *-ly → -ment*, *-ous → -eux*, *-ical → -ique*) and beware the famous false friends: *actuellement* (currently), *librairie* (bookshop), *blessé* (injured), *sensible* (sensitive), *attendre* (to wait), *rester* (to stay), *une journée* (a day's length, not a journey), *un coin* (a corner), *un magasin* (a shop).
2. **Spelling is history, not sound.** French spelling records the language of the thirteenth century; the sound moved on. Most final consonants are silent, *-ent* on a verb is silent, four or five spellings share one sound (*o, au, eau*; *é, er, ez, et*), and one spelling can be two words (*fils*, son or threads; *est*, is or east). The good news is that the rules from spelling to sound are a closed set (Block 1) and hold for life; the reverse direction, from sound to spelling, is the lifelong *dictée* problem, because *parler, parlé, parlez, parlais* all sound alike.
3. **Two genders, few clues.** Masculine (*le livre*) and feminine (*la table*). Endings help about nine times in ten (*-tion, -té, -ure, -ette* feminine; *-age, -ment, -eau, -isme* masculine), but *le problème, la peau* and hundreds of others must be learned. Learn the article with every noun, always.
4. **Everything agrees, mostly silently.** Articles, adjectives, possessives and past participles change to match gender and number: *les petites maisons blanches, elles sont parties*. In speech the agreement is often inaudible (*petit / petits* sound the same) and sometimes audible (*petit / petite*); in writing it is always there and always judged.
5. **The verb carries the person, but the pronoun stays.** Each person has its own written ending (*je parle, tu parles, il parle, nous parlons, vous parlez, ils parlent*), but four of the six sound identical, so French, unlike Spanish or Italian, never drops the subject pronoun. Verb tables are still the backbone of the programme: three regular groups and about forty irregulars that cover most of speech.
6. **Being and having.** One verb *être* for identity, state and location (*Je suis anglais. Elle est fatiguée. Il est à Paris.*), plus *c'est* for pointing at and commenting (*C'est mon frère. C'est bon.*) and *il y a* for existence (*Il y a un café.*). What English "is", French often "has": *avoir faim, avoir vingt ans, avoir raison*.
7. **Questions three ways, negation in two pieces.** Intonation (*Tu viens ?*), *est-ce que* (*Est-ce que tu viens ?*) or inversion (*Viens-tu ?*) all make a question. Negation wraps the verb in *ne ... pas* (*Je ne sais pas*); speech drops the *ne* (*Je sais pas*), writing never does. *Jamais, rien, personne, plus* replace *pas*.
8. **One past tense at A1, two at A2.** The *passé composé* (*j'ai mangé, je suis allé*) covers both "I ate" and "I have eaten" and is the only past tense you need to produce at A1. A small list of verbs of movement and all reflexives take *être* instead of *avoir*, and then the participle agrees. The *imparfait* for background and habit arrives at A2; the written-only *passé simple* you learn to recognise at B1.
9. **Articles everywhere, and the partitive.** French nouns almost never stand bare: *J'aime le fromage. Je mange du fromage. Je n'ai pas de fromage.* The partitive *du / de la / des* ("some") collapses to *de* after a negative and after quantities (*beaucoup de*). *À* and *de* fuse with *le* and *les* (*au, aux, du, des*).
10. **Sound is the first filter.** Four nasal vowels, a front rounded *u* against *ou*, an *r* in the throat, a mute *e* that comes and goes, and liaison, which pronounces a silent consonant before a vowel and glues the phrase together (*les amis, vous êtes, un homme*). Every syllable is even and only the last of a group is stressed. A learner who has these is understood through any amount of grammar error; one who has not is not understood at all.
11. **Numbers count by twenties.** 70 is *soixante-dix*, 80 *quatre-vingts*, 90 *quatre-vingt-dix* in France and Québec; Belgium and Switzerland say *septante* and *nonante* and part of Switzerland *huitante*. The decimal separator is a comma, the thousands separator a space (*1 500,50 €*), and phone numbers are read in pairs.
12. **Politeness has a grammar.** *Tu* for family, friends, children and fellow students; *vous* for strangers, shops, officials, anyone older or senior, and every group. *Bonjour* opens every exchange (walking into a shop without it is rude), *Monsieur / Madame* follows it, *s'il vous plaît* and *merci* close it; *je voudrais* replaces *je veux* when asking for anything. The register gap between speech (*on, je sais pas, bouquin*) and writing (*nous, je ne sais pas, livre*) is wide and both are learned.

## Appendix B: A1 grammar checklist

By the end of A1 you must be able to produce all of the following correctly and without hesitation.

1. **Noun gender and articles**: *le / la / l' / les*; *un / une / des*; the ending clues (*-tion, -té, -ure, -ette* f.; *-age, -ment, -eau, -isme* m.) and the exceptions *le problème, le musée, le lycée, la peau, l'eau, la plage*; *au, aux, du, des* contractions.
2. **Plurals**: *-s* (silent); *-s, -x, -z* unchanged; *-eau, -eu → -x*; *-al → -aux*; *l'œil / les yeux*; the plural heard in the article and the liaison (*les amis*).
3. **Adjective agreement and position**: *grand / grande / grands / grandes*; *-eux / -euse, -f / -ve, -er / -ère, -en / -enne*; *beau / bel / belle, nouveau / nouvel / nouvelle, vieux / vieil / vieille, blanc / blanche, long / longue, gros / grosse, gentil / gentille*; after the noun by default, before it for *beau, joli, jeune, vieux, nouveau, bon, mauvais, grand, petit, gros, autre, même*; *de* before a plural adjective.
4. **The partitive and *de***: *du, de la, de l', des*; *de* after negation (*pas de*) and quantities (*beaucoup de, un peu de, trop de, assez de, un kilo de, un verre de, combien de*); *le / la / les* after *aimer, adorer, détester, préférer*.
5. ***C'est* vs *il est*, and the idioms**: *c'est* + determiner + noun, name, pronoun or general comment; *il est* + adjective or bare profession or nationality; *avoir faim, soif, chaud, froid, peur, sommeil, raison, tort, besoin de, envie de, mal à, X ans*; *être en retard, d'accord, en train de*.
6. **Present tense, regular *-er***: *parle, parles, parle, parlons, parlez, parlent*; *nous mangeons, nous commençons, j'achète, je préfère, j'appelle, je paie*.
7. **Irregular verbs, 1**: *être, avoir, aller, faire, prendre (apprendre, comprendre), venir (tenir, devenir), pouvoir, vouloir (je voudrais), devoir, savoir, connaître*.
8. **Irregular verbs, 2, and the regular *-ir* / *-re* groups**: *finir (-iss-), vendre (attendre, entendre, répondre, perdre, descendre), voir, dire (vous dites), lire, écrire, mettre, partir, sortir, dormir, boire*.
9. **Possessives, demonstratives and *quel***: *mon / ma / mes ... leur / leurs* agreeing with the thing; *mon amie*; *ce / cet / cette / ces*; *-ci / -là*; *ça*; *quel / quelle / quels / quelles*.
10. **Negation**: *ne ... pas, ne ... jamais, ne ... rien, ne ... personne, ne ... plus*; *n'* before a vowel; *pas de*; the *ne* heard dropped in speech and never dropped in writing.
11. **Questions**: intonation, *est-ce que*, inversion with *-t-*; *qui, que / qu'est-ce que / quoi, où, d'où, quand, comment, pourquoi, combien (de), quel*.
12. **Reflexive verbs**: *se lever (me, te, se, nous, vous, se; m', t', s')*, *s'appeler, se réveiller, se laver, se doucher, s'habiller, se coucher, s'endormir, se promener, se reposer, s'amuser, se dépêcher*; *Je ne me lève pas*; *Je vais me coucher*.
13. **Verb + infinitive**: *aller* + infinitive for the future; *venir de* + infinitive; *pouvoir, vouloir, devoir, il faut, aimer, préférer* + infinitive.
14. **One past tense**: the *passé composé* with *avoir* (*-é, -i, -u*; irregular *été, eu, fait, pris, mis, vu, dit, lu, écrit, bu, pu, voulu, dû, su, connu, ouvert*) and with *être* for *aller, venir, arriver, partir, entrer, sortir, monter, descendre, rester, tomber, naître, mourir, retourner, passer* and their compounds, and for all reflexives, with agreement of the participle; *hier, la semaine dernière, il y a deux jours, déjà, pas encore, jamais*.
15. **Time, date, weather**: *Il est trois heures et demie / moins le quart*; the 24-hour clock; *le lundi* vs *lundi*; months; *le 3 mai, le premier janvier*; seasons with *au / en*; *premier ... dixième*; *aujourd'hui, hier, demain, ce soir, le week-end, la semaine prochaine*; *Il fait beau / chaud / froid / du vent, il pleut, il neige, il y a des nuages*.
16. **Prepositions and place**: *à, de, en, au / aux, dans, sur, sous, devant, derrière, entre, chez, avec, sans, pour, par, vers, jusqu'à, depuis, pendant, avant, après*; *à côté de, en face de, près de, loin de, à gauche / droite de, au bout de, au coin de, au milieu de*; *tout droit*; *il y a* vs *est / se trouve*; *en France, au Canada, aux États-Unis, à Paris*.
17. **Numbers, quantity and connectors**: 0–1000 with the *soixante-dix / quatre-vingts* system and *septante / nonante* recognised; prices and the decimal comma; *très, beaucoup, un peu, trop, assez, peu, quelques, plusieurs, tout / toute / tous / toutes*; *et, ou, mais, parce que, donc, alors, puis, ensuite, après, d'abord, enfin, aussi, quand, si, comme, par exemple*; the common imperatives you hear constantly, *regarde, écoute, attends, viens, va, dis-moi, allez, tiens, pardon*.

## Appendix G: Pronunciation word list (40 words)

Nasal vowel sets (4 sets, 12 words): *vent / vin / vont · banc / bain / bon · sans / saint / son · an / un / on*. Four distinct nasal vowels (the *un* of *un* may merge with *vin* in a Paris accent, but never with *on*).
Nasal against non-nasal (2 pairs, 4 words): *bon / bonne · plein / pleine*. The second of each pair has a real *n* and no nasal vowel.
*u / ou* pairs (5 pairs, 10 words): *tu / tout · rue / roue · pur / pour · vu / vous · dessus / dessous*.
The *r* (6): *rouge · Paris · pour · très · quatre · merci*. In the throat, never the English *r*; the clusters *très, quatre* are the test.
Liaison, elision and the mute final *e* (4 items, 4 words counted as one each): *les amis* (with *z*) · *vous êtes* (with *z*) · *un homme* (with *n*) · *petit / petite* (the *t* heard only in the second).
Vowels and rhythm (4): *été* (closed *é*, no glide) · *père* (open *è*) · *sœur* (open *eu*) · *université* (five even syllables, stress on the last).
